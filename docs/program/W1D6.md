## Chapter 1 · Day six (0:00)

[warmly] Hello. Day six.

Nearly a week. You might be starting to feel it. Longer stretches where you don't think about it at all. A bit of pride, quietly.

And that's exactly when a particular passenger tends to speak up. You met it on the night of Day Xero. Today it gets its own session, because it's the most convincing one on the bus.

## Chapter 2 · The clever one (0:28)

It says, "Just one."

And notice how well it argues. [pause] "You've done nearly a week. You've proved you can stop. So one won't matter. You've earned it. You can stop again tomorrow."

It takes the thing you're proud of and uses it as the reason to undo it.

Here's what it leaves out. The gap has been closing for six days. One {s: cigarette | v: hit | p: pouch} doesn't feed a pleasure. It reopens the gap. And then the gap asks again, a little while later, with a much better argument, because now it's "just one more."

It isn't really offering you one. It's offering you the first one.

## Chapter 3 · Play the tape (1:15)

[calmly] So when it shows up, you don't argue. You play the tape forward. Let's do that now, slowly.

Take a breath, in a way that feels comfortable for you. [long pause]

Imagine you said yes. Picture the first few seconds. [long pause] Be honest about them. There might be relief.

Now keep the tape running. Ten minutes later. What are you feeling? [long pause]

That evening. Is it one? [long pause]

The next morning, when you wake up. What's the first thing you want? [long pause]

And a week from then. Where are you? [long pause]

Now come back. Right here, six days in, with none of that having happened. [long pause] Notice how that feels in your body. That steadiness is yours.

The thought only ever shows you the first few seconds. You've just watched the whole thing.

## Chapter 4 · Your own evidence (2:38)

And you don't have to take my word for what those first few seconds are worth. You checked.

In the prep week, there was one you had with your full attention. Before it, you guessed how good it would be. Afterwards, you said how good it actually was. [pause] In a moment, both of those answers will be on your screen. Have a look at the distance between them. That's what "just one" is selling.

One honest thing before you go. If you ever do have one, a slip doesn't undo anything. The most useful thing is to get straight back to your plan, right now, not tomorrow. Most people who quit for good had slips, or several tries, along the way. But you don't need a slip to learn this lesson. You already ran the experiment.

So today, when "just one" turns up, greet it like someone you recognize. Then play the tape to the end.

[warmly] Tomorrow is one week. I'll see you there.

## Step 3 · Myth or fact · Just one (3 min)

### Statement 1
"Now that I've done nearly a week, one wouldn't hurt." · Answer: **Myth** · Verdict: It's the first one, not just one. · Explanation: In a large study of people quitting smoking, using at all in the first two weeks was one of the strongest signs of going back to using every day. One reopens the gap, and the gap asks again. · Source: Kenford, JAMA 1994

### Statement 2
"If I do have one, my progress in DayXero goes back to zero." · Answer: **Myth** · Verdict: Your days stay. · Explanation: A slip is one moment, not a verdict. Your days, money kept and cravings ridden out all stay. You log the slip, the app helps you look at what happened, and you carry on from where you are. · Source: none (how DayXero works)

### Statement 3
"Most people who quit for good had slips or several tries along the way." · Answer: **Fact** · Verdict: It's the usual road. · Explanation: Stopping for good often takes more than one attempt, and each one teaches something. That isn't a reason to slip. It's a reason not to treat a slip, if one happens, as the end of the story. · Source: Chaiton, BMJ Open 2016
