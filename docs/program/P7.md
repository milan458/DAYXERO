### Script (Eleven v4)

Voice direction: Warm, steady and unhurried, like a counselor who's on your side; gentle on the fears, plain and confident on the facts, never cheerleading. About 130 words a minute, with real space in the pauses.

## Chapter 1 · The fear, out loud (0:00)

[warmly] Tomorrow is Day Xero. And if part of you feels uneasy about that this morning, I'd like to start right there. [pause]

Maybe there's a tightness when you think about tomorrow. A quiet voice saying "not yet." Or a list of reasons this might be a bad week to stop. [pause] That's normal. Almost everyone feels some version of it the day before.

Here's what I'd like you to see. That fear isn't a sign you're not ready. It's one of the ways nicotine keeps people using. Think about it. If stopping felt like nothing, nobody would stay stuck for years. The dread of going without is part of how the addiction protects itself. [pause] So of course it's loud today. Tomorrow is the day it stops being fed.

You don't have to argue with the fear, and you don't have to wait for it to leave. You can just notice it. "There's the fear." [pause] And then look at what it's actually saying.

So let's do that. We'll take the big fears one at a time, with our eyes open.

## Chapter 2 · "I'll miss it" (1:30)

The first one usually sounds like this: I'll miss it.

So what, exactly, would you miss? [pause] All week you've been watching closely. You've seen the gap: that restless, slightly empty feeling that builds between doses. And you've seen that most of what feels good about {s: a cigarette | v: the vape | p: a pouch} is that gap closing.

People who don't use nicotine don't have the gap. They don't spend the day topping up a feeling the last dose left behind. So what you'd miss isn't a pleasure they're going without. It's relief from something they never feel. [pause]

That's why tomorrow isn't a sacrifice. You're not giving anything up. You're putting down something that's been quietly taking from you, many times a day. [pause] Free, not deprived.

Then there's the fear underneath it: I'll be miserable. Here I want to be straight with you. The first few days can be uncomfortable. Cravings come. You might feel snappy, restless, foggy or hungrier than usual. Sleep might be off for a bit. That's withdrawal, and it's real.

But it has an end. For most people it's strongest in the first week, and most of it eases within two to four weeks. [pause] Discomfort that has an end is very different from misery. And every craving you ride out is one fewer ahead of you.

## Chapter 3 · Your break, and your weight (3:20)

Next: I'll lose my break.

Here's a question. Is it the nicotine you'd miss, or the five minutes? [pause] Stepping outside. Leaving the desk. A breath of air. A moment that belongs to you.

You keep all of that. The break was never the problem. You can still step out, stand in the sun, take five minutes with a drink or a friend. The only part that's leaving is the part that brought the gap back an hour later. [pause] So tomorrow, take your breaks. Just take them as someone who doesn't need anything from them.

And then, for a lot of people, the quiet one: I'll gain weight.

It's a fair worry, so here are the honest facts. Some weight gain after quitting is common. A large review of studies in the BMJ, looking at people who quit smoking, found the average was about nine to eleven pounds over the first year. But the range was wide. Some people gained very little, and about one in six lost weight. [pause] It isn't a given. Regular meals, water close by and something crunchy for when your hands want something all go a long way. We'll come back to them.

## Chapter 4 · Who am I without it? (5:00)

The last fear is the deepest one. Who am I without it?

Maybe it's been part of your day for years. The first thing in the morning. Something to do with your hands. How you pause, how you celebrate, how you get through the hard bits. It can feel like part of who you are. [pause]

But think back. There was a version of you before you ever {s: lit one | v: took a puff | p: put one in}. That person laughed, worked, got bored, got through hard days. Nothing essential about you came from nicotine. [pause]

What you care about is still yours. Think of the reason you chose at the start of the week, the one that matters most. Hold it for a moment now. [long pause] That reason says far more about who you are than a habit ever did.

Tomorrow you don't become someone else. You become someone who doesn't need it.

## Chapter 5 · Today and tonight (6:15)

So here's where we are. [pause] The fears are real feelings. But look closely, and most of them are the addiction talking. You'd miss the relief, not a pleasure. Your breaks stay. Weight is manageable. You stay you. [pause]

When one of those thoughts comes back today, and it might, try saying it this way: "I'm having the thought that I'll miss it." Then let it sit there. A thought isn't an order. [pause]

For now, use as normal. Later, you'll clear things out and write a line to tomorrow-you. And this evening I'll be with you for the last one, with your eyes fully open. [pause]

[softly] One more day of seeing clearly. Then you get to stop.

## Step 2 · Reveal · What the first week can feel like (5 min)

Intro line: "Withdrawal is real, and it has an end. Here's what to expect, honestly, and what helps."

Each card back shows the answer, any timeline chips (when · title, quoted from `health-timeline.json`) and the source line. "Go deeper" opens a sheet.

### Card 1

**Front:** When does it start, and when is it worst?

**Answer:** It can start within a few hours of your last {product}. For most people it's strongest in the first days to a week, then it weakens the longer you go without.
Timeline chips: **A few hours** · Withdrawal can start · **First week** · Withdrawal is usually at its strongest

**Go deeper:** Nicotine doesn't last long in the body. Its half-life is about two hours, so the level falls steadily between doses. That falling level is the gap you've watched all week, and it's why the first one of the morning feels so strong. From tomorrow nothing refills it. Within a few hours your brain notices, and that's when the first cravings, restlessness or a short fuse tend to show up. It's uncomfortable, and it's also the sign your body has started adjusting. The first days are the steepest part for most people, which is why your support is front-loaded: Craving SOS, your plans, and a short session every day.

*Source: smokefree.gov (Nicotine withdrawal); Benowitz, NEJM 2010*

### Card 2

**Front:** What might I actually feel?

**Answer:** Cravings that come in waves. Irritable or restless. Low or anxious. Harder to focus. Hungrier. Sleep a bit off. Not everyone gets all of these. They're signs of adjusting.

**Go deeper:** These are the symptoms that research has confirmed as genuine nicotine withdrawal. Cravings rise, peak and fall, usually within minutes. Irritability is one of the most common, so it's worth warning the people around you. Low mood or anxiety can show up early; for most people, **1 to 2 weeks · Mood changes from withdrawal usually ease**. If low mood hangs around longer than that, talk to a doctor. You don't have to push through it alone. Focus can feel foggy for a few days, so take the day in small pieces. Hunger is common: keep easy snacks around. Sleep can be restless at first and usually settles.

*Source: Hughes, Nicotine Tob Res 2007; smokefree.gov (Managing nicotine withdrawal)*

### Card 3

**Front:** How long does it last?

**Answer:** Most symptoms ease within two to four weeks. Cravings can last a little longer, but they come less often and feel weaker as the weeks go by.
Timeline chips: **1 to 2 weeks** · Mood changes from withdrawal usually ease · **A few weeks** · Cravings come less often

**Go deeper:** Think of it as a slope, not a wall. The first week is the climb. After that, most of the physical side settles, usually within two to four weeks. Cravings are the part that can stay a little longer: "Cravings can last longer than the physical symptoms, but they come less often and feel weaker as the weeks go by." That's why the program keeps going after Day Xero, with skills for the moments that still turn up. Every wave you ride is practice your brain keeps. Tonight it can feel as if the hard part will last forever. It won't. It has a shape, and you'll be watching it get smaller.

*Source: Hughes, Nicotine Tob Res 2007; smokefree.gov (Nicotine withdrawal)*

### Card 4

**Front:** What actually helps?

**Answer:** Riding each wave instead of fighting it. Food, water and sleep. Moving a little. Telling people. NRT, if you've chosen it. And Craving SOS, one tap away.

**Go deeper:** Match the help to the feeling. A craving: notice it, name it, breathe slowly and ride it, or open Craving SOS. Irritable: tell people ahead of time, and try two slow sighs (two breaths in through the nose, one long breath out) before you answer. Foggy: short lists, one task at a time, a walk outside. Hungry: regular meals, water first, crunchy snacks. Sleep: coffee before noon, screens down earlier. {s: And halve your coffee for now: after you stop smoking, the same cup hits harder. | v: Keep an eye on how much coffee you drink if sleep is off. | p: Keep an eye on how much coffee you drink if sleep is off.} If you're using NRT, a patch plus a fast-acting form works better than one alone. Follow the pack.

*Source: Balban, Cell Rep Med 2023; Cochrane review of combination NRT, 2019{s: ; Benowitz, Hall & Modin, BMJ 1989 | v: | p: }*

## Step 3 · Myth or fact · Fears (5 min)

Intro line: "Five things people fear the night before. Tap Myth or Fact."

### Statement 1

"If I quit, I'll definitely gain a lot of weight."

**Answer:** Myth
**Verdict:** Some gain is common. A lot isn't a given.
**Explanation:** It's a fair worry, and the honest answer is in between. A large review found people who quit smoking gained about 4 to 5 kg (9 to 11 lb) on average over the first year, with a wide range: some gained little, and about 16 in 100 lost weight. Hunger is a normal part of withdrawal, so plan for it: regular meals, water close by, something crunchy for your hands. {s: | v: That research looked at people who smoked. There's less on vaping. | p: That research looked at people who smoked. There's less on pouches.}
**Source:** Aubin et al., BMJ 2012; Hughes, Nicotine Tob Res 2007

### Statement 2

"Withdrawal lasts for months."

**Answer:** Myth
**Verdict:** For most people, the worst is over in weeks.
**Explanation:** Withdrawal is usually strongest in the first week. Most symptoms, like irritability, restlessness and trouble focusing, ease within two to four weeks. Cravings can hang around a little longer, but they come less often and feel weaker as the weeks go by. So the hard part has an end, and it's nearer than it feels tonight. Each wave you ride brings it closer.
**Source:** Hughes, Nicotine Tob Res 2007; smokefree.gov (health timeline: "A few weeks · Cravings come less often")

### Statement 3

"I'll never enjoy a coffee or a drink again."

**Answer:** Myth
**Verdict:** The link is learned, and it can loosen.
**Explanation:** Coffee and nicotine feel joined because they've happened together thousands of times. The first few coffees without it may feel strange, and that's the old pairing showing itself, not something missing from the coffee. {s: One real change: after you stop smoking, caffeine hits harder, so try half your usual for a while. | v: Give it some time. | p: Give it some time.} In studies of people who quit smoking, mood and quality of life went up on average, not down.
**Source:** Taylor et al., BMJ 2014{s: ; Benowitz, Hall & Modin, BMJ 1989 | v: | p: }

### Statement 4

"I'll always miss it."

**Answer:** Myth
**Verdict:** Missing it fades as the gap does.
**Explanation:** What people miss at first is mostly relief: the gap closing. Once nicotine stops arriving, the gap stops being refilled, and cravings come less often and feel weaker as the weeks go by. Wanting it was never proof that it gave you something. It was proof that it took something first. You don't have to feel nothing to be free. You just have to keep letting the waves pass.
**Source:** smokefree.gov (health timeline: "A few weeks · Cravings come less often"); Benowitz, NEJM 2010
