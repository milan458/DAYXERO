### Script (Eleven v4)

Voice direction: warm, calm and unhurried, like a trusted counselor on a first meeting; gentle confidence, low energy, a real smile in chapter 1 and a slower, quieter pace in chapter 3.

## Chapter 1 · You don't have to quit today (0:00)

[warmly] Hi. I'm really glad you're here. [pause]

For the next seven days, I'll be with you for a few minutes in the morning and a few minutes in the evening. And I want to start with the thing that surprises most people. [pause] You don't have to quit today. You don't have to quit tomorrow, either.

For the whole of this week, you keep {s: smoking | v: vaping | p: using pouches} exactly as you do now. Same amount, same times, same places. No cutting down. No counting the hours with your jaw clenched.

That might sound odd, coming from a program that's meant to help you stop. So here's why. [pause] It's very hard to look clearly at something while you're fighting it. When you try to stop before you understand what's going on, every {s: cigarette | v: puff | p: pouch} you can't have starts to look more precious, not less. So this week, we look first. We take the habit apart, piece by piece, while it's still right there in your hand. Then, on your Quit Day, you stop. Not because you've run out of reasons to keep going, but because you've seen what it really was.

You might feel some relief hearing that. You might feel a bit of doubt, too. Both are fine. You can be unsure and still be here. And if part of you is thinking, "I'll believe it when I see it," good. That's exactly the attitude this week needs.

## Chapter 2 · This was never about willpower (1:30)

Maybe you've tried before. Maybe more than once. If it didn't last, I want to say this clearly. [pause] That doesn't mean you're weak.

Nicotine is highly addictive. It works fast, it wears off fast, and it teaches your brain to want it back, over and over, many times a day. That's not a flaw in you. It's how the drug works.

And trying to quit on willpower alone is a hard way to go about it. A review of studies on people who tried to quit without any help found that only about three to five in every hundred of those attempts lasted six months to a year. [pause] Hear that the right way. It doesn't mean quitting is rare. Millions of people have quit. It means that gritting your teeth, on your own, against something you don't fully understand, is a poor tool for the job.

So we're going to use better tools. Understanding first. Then skills for the hard moments. Then setting up your space and your support. People who stay quit aren't made of stronger stuff. They tend to see it differently, and they're better prepared. That's what this week is for.

## Chapter 3 · The gap (3:00)

[calmly] Let me show you the first piece. I call it the gap.

Nicotine leaves your body fairly quickly. In about two hours, roughly half of it is gone. And as the level drops, a feeling arrives. [pause] It's a little restless. A little empty. Slightly on edge, as if something isn't quite finished. It's hard to settle, hard to focus.

It's mild. So mild that most people never put a name to it. It just feels like, "I could do with one." And because it comes back every hour or two, all day long, it starts to feel like your normal. Like that's just how you are.

Then you {s: light up | v: take a hit | p: put one in}, and within {s: seconds | v: seconds | p: a few minutes}, that feeling settles. And the settling feels good. It feels like pleasure, or calm, or focus. [pause] That relief is real. I'm not going to tell you you're imagining it.

But here's the part I'd like you to sit with. What it relieves is the gap the last one opened.

Think of a tap dripping in another room. After a while you stop hearing it, but part of you stays a little tense. When someone finally turns it off, the quiet feels wonderful. [pause] But a person in a house with no dripping tap already has that quiet. All day long. They don't feel it as a treat, because nothing was missing.

People who don't use nicotine don't have the gap. They aren't holding back or going without. There's simply nothing pulling at them. [pause] And that's where you're heading. Not to a life with something missing. To a life without the gap.

## Chapter 4 · Today: just watch (4:50)

So, today. There's one thing I'd like you to do, and it's small. [pause]

Keep using as normal. But a few times today, before you {s: light up | v: take a hit | p: put one in}, take one slow breath and ask yourself two questions. How much of this is the gap? And where do I feel it? Your chest, your throat, your hands, your head. [pause] There are no right answers. Some will feel mostly like the gap. Some won't. You're just collecting evidence. If you notice the gap, good. If you can't find it, that's useful too. Either way, you've looked, and looking is the whole job today.

You don't have to believe a word I've said yet. I'd much rather you find out for yourself.

In a moment, you'll set up three of these check-ins, and bring your reasons along with you. And this evening, there's a short track to look back on what you noticed.

[warmly] That's it for this morning. Keep using. Just watch.

## Step 2 · Reveal · Why the first one feels so good (5 min)

**Intro line:** "Four questions about that first one. Tap each card to turn it over."

### Card 1

**Front:** Why does the first one of the day hit so hard?

**Answer:** Because you wake up in the gap. A night without nicotine leaves the level low, so the first {s: cigarette | v: hit | p: pouch} has the most to close. That's why it feels like the best one.

**Go deeper:** Nicotine wears off quickly: about half of it is gone in two hours or so. After seven or eight hours of sleep, very little is left. You don't feel the gap much while you're asleep, so you wake up in the middle of it, often without noticing. Then the first one of the day closes the widest gap you'll have all day, all at once. The relief is bigger, so it feels like the best one. It isn't adding more than the others. There's just more to fill. Tomorrow you'll look at the other half of the story: the morning routine that comes with it.

*Source: Benowitz, 2010*

### Card 2

**Front:** How does someone who doesn't use nicotine wake up?

**Answer:** Without the gap. Their morning can still be tired, rushed or grumpy, but nothing is tugging at them, and there's no sense of something they need to sort out first.

**Go deeper:** It's easy to assume that people who don't use are going without something every morning. They aren't. They never built the gap, so there's nothing to close. They have the same alarms, the same tired mornings, the same coffee. What they don't have is an extra layer of restlessness waiting to be fixed before the day can start. That's worth picturing, because it's where you're heading. Not a morning with something missing. A morning without the tug. Many people who quit say mornings were one of the first things that felt different, in a good way. You'll find out for yourself.

*Source: Benowitz, 2010 (withdrawal builds between doses in people who are dependent)*

### Card 3

**Front:** If it's just the gap, why does it feel like it helps?

**Answer:** Because relief is a real feeling. Going from on edge to settled feels good, it happens quickly, and it happens many times a day. Your brain files that as "this helps."

**Go deeper:** Relief is one of the most convincing feelings there is. You feel a bit off, you use, you feel better, and the timing is so neat that the conclusion seems obvious. Now repeat that a dozen times a day. That's more than four thousand times a year, each one a small lesson that says "this is what makes me feel right." Nobody would come away from that much practice believing anything else. So it isn't foolish to feel that it helps. The feeling is honest. It's the explanation underneath it that's worth checking: was it adding something, or ending something it started?

### Card 4

**Front:** Do I have to stop believing it helps, starting today?

**Answer:** No. You can hold both. It feels like it helps, and you're curious what it's actually helping with. Beliefs loosen with evidence, not force. This week is for gathering it.

**Go deeper:** You can't argue yourself out of a feeling, and you don't need to. This week, thoughts like "I need one" or "this is the only thing that settles me" will keep showing up. Let them. You might even say to yourself, "I'm having the thought that I need one." That small step back turns a command into something you can look at. Meanwhile, you keep using as normal and you keep watching. Each check-in, each moment you notice the gap closing, is a piece of your own evidence. By Day Xero the aim is that you see it differently because you've seen it for yourself, not because anyone told you to.

## Step 3 · Myth or fact · What you've been told (6 min)

**Intro line:** "Five things people often believe about nicotine. Tap Myth or Fact. There's no score."

### Statement 1

**Statement:** "I {s: smoke | v: vape | p: use pouches} because I enjoy it."

**Answer:** Partly true

**Verdict:** The relief is real. Mostly, it's the gap closing.

**Explanation:** You're not imagining the good feeling, and moments like a break or a chat can be genuinely pleasant. But nicotine wears off quickly, and as it does it leaves a restless, slightly empty feeling. Using ends that feeling within {s: seconds | v: seconds | p: minutes}, and the ending feels like pleasure. People who don't use don't have that feeling to end. Your check-ins today are a way to test this for yourself.

**Source:** Benowitz, 2010

### Statement 2

**Statement:** "It's just a habit."

**Answer:** Myth

**Verdict:** It's a habit and a drug, working together.

**Explanation:** The habit part is real: the coffee, the car, the break at work. But nicotine is also a drug your brain adapts to, so when the level drops you feel a pull to top it up. The habit decides when; the drug makes you want it. That's why trying harder often isn't enough on its own, and why this week works on both: the beliefs behind the wanting, and the moments that set it off.

**Source:** Benowitz, 2010

### Statement 3

**Statement:** "I could stop any time I wanted to."

**Answer:** Myth

**Verdict:** Nicotine makes stopping harder than it sounds.

**Explanation:** It's natural to feel in control, especially on a day when you're not trying to stop. But most quit attempts are made without any help, and very few of those last: in one review, only about 3–5% lasted six to twelve months. That isn't a judgment on anyone's strength. It shows how strongly nicotine pulls, and why understanding, skills and support make a real difference.

**Source:** Hughes, Keely & Naud, 2004; Babb et al., 2017

### Statement 4

**Statement:** "Most people who smoke want to quit."

**Answer:** Fact

**Verdict:** About two in three adults who smoke want to.

**Explanation:** In a large US survey, 68% of adults who smoke said they wanted to stop completely. So if part of you wants out while you're still using, you're not unusual or inconsistent. Wanting to stop and still reaching for the next one is exactly how nicotine works. {s: You're in good company. | v: That survey asked about cigarettes, and there's less data on vaping so far, but you're in good company. | p: That survey asked about cigarettes, and there's less data on pouches so far, but you're in good company.}

**Source:** Babb et al., 2017

### Statement 5

**Statement:** "It hasn't harmed me yet, so I'm fine."

**Answer:** Myth

**Verdict:** {s: Harm builds quietly. So does recovery. | v: The long-term picture isn't known yet. | p: The long-term picture isn't known yet.}

**Explanation (smoking):** Most of the harm from smoking builds slowly over years, which is why people often feel fine for a long time. The hopeful side works the same way. Your heart rate and blood pressure start to come down within minutes of your last cigarette, and quitting at any age adds years of life, more the earlier you stop. You don't need to feel unwell to have a good reason.

**Source (smoking):** Doll et al., 2004; American Cancer Society (health timeline)

**Explanation (vaping and pouches):** {v: Vapes | p: Pouches} haven't been around long enough for anyone to know the long-term picture. Most of the harm from tobacco comes from the smoke, so the risks are likely to be different. But the nicotine still keeps you dependent: the gap every hour or two, the money, the planning around the next one. You don't need to feel unwell to have a good reason to be free of that.

**Source (vaping and pouches):** Royal College of Physicians, 2016; Benowitz, 2010

## Chapter 1 · Settle in (0:00)

[softly] Hi again. Before we start, find somewhere you can be still for a few minutes. Sit back, or lie down if you'd like. [pause]

Take one slow breath in through your nose, and let it go, a little longer on the way out. [long pause]

Let your shoulders drop a little. Let your jaw soften. There's nothing you need to do for the next few minutes except listen.

This morning I asked you to keep using as normal, and to watch. However that went, you've come back for the second half of the day. That counts for more than you might think.

## Chapter 2 · What you noticed (1:00)

Let's look back. Your check-ins may be on the screen in front of you. Or you can simply remember. [pause]

Maybe the gap was easy to find. A restless edge in your chest. A fidget in your hands. A sense that the moment wasn't quite finished until you'd {s: lit up | v: had a hit | p: put one in}. If so, you've just seen something most people who use never notice. [pause] And if most of your ratings were high, if most of them felt like the gap, that isn't bad news. It means the thing you've been reaching for is mostly fixing a feeling it caused. That's something you can step out of.

Maybe it was harder to find. That's useful too. The gap is quiet. It sits in the background, like that dripping tap, and you've lived with it for so long it feels like part of the furniture. Often you only notice it in the moment it stops, in those first few {s: seconds | v: seconds | p: minutes} after you use.

Maybe some of them felt like real enjoyment, not relief at all. That's fine. Hold on to that. In a couple of days we'll look at enjoyment closely, with real curiosity, and not to argue you out of anything.

And maybe you forgot to check in at all. [light laugh] That's worth noticing too. The fact that a {s: cigarette | v: vape | p: pouch} can come and go without you really clocking it tells you something. Tomorrow is all about that. The autopilot.

Whatever you found, there's no score here. The only aim today was to look.

## Chapter 3 · Two minds (3:00)

[gently] There's something else that often shows up on the first day, and I want to name it.

Part of you wants to stop. That's why you're here. And part of you doesn't, or isn't sure, or feels a small knot of worry at the thought of life without it. [pause]

Both parts make sense. Mixed feelings aren't a sign that you're not ready. They're what it feels like to be in the middle of changing something that's been with you for a long time. They're normal.

Here's something worth knowing about that worry. A lot of it comes from the addiction itself. The same drug that opens the gap every hour or two also makes the thought of never closing it again feel alarming. So the fear isn't proof that you need it. It's one more part of the pull. [pause]

When thoughts like "I could never" or "I'll be miserable" show up this week, you don't have to argue with them. Just notice them. You could even say, quietly, "I'm having the thought that I could never." A thought is something passing through. It isn't a forecast, and it isn't an order. You can let it be there, and still choose what you do next.

## Chapter 4 · Your reason (4:40)

Earlier today you chose the reason that matters most to you. Bring it to mind now. [pause]

Not as a rule you have to live up to. More like a direction. A compass doesn't push you anywhere. It just shows you which way is north.

Picture one specific moment your reason points to. Somewhere you'd be. Someone you'd be with. What you'd be doing. Maybe it's keeping up with someone you love, without stopping to catch your breath. Maybe it's waking up without the tug. Maybe it's something only you know. [long pause]

Notice how that moment feels. [pause] That's what this week is about. Not giving something up. Moving toward something you want. Whenever this week gets confusing, or the doubt gets loud, come back to that picture. It'll be waiting.

## Chapter 5 · Tonight (5:50)

For the rest of this evening, carry on as normal. If you use before bed, you might notice the gap one more time: the restless edge, and then the settling. Just notice it. There's nothing to fix.

Then sleep. Tomorrow morning, you'll wake up after a long stretch without nicotine, which means you'll wake up in the gap. [pause] Don't change anything. Just notice the first one of the day, and what comes right before it. Tomorrow we'll look at why that one feels so strong, and how so many of them happen without you deciding at all.

Before you go, one more slow breath. In through your nose. [pause] And a long, easy breath out. [long pause]

If you'd like, play this morning's track again tomorrow, or any time. Hearing an idea twice helps it settle.

[warmly] You've done your first day. You looked. That's the beginning of seeing it clearly. Sleep well.

## Claims to verify

1. **Speed of the settling by product** (Step 1 ch. 3, Myth 1, Step 5 ch. 2): "within seconds" for cigarettes and vapes, "within a few minutes" for pouches. Benowitz 2010 covers fast delivery from smoking; vapes and oral pouches need a source on absorption speed (e.g. a pharmacokinetics study of e-cigarettes and of nicotine pouches). If not confirmed, change all three variants to "soon after".
2. **"Many people who quit say mornings were one of the first things that felt different"** (Reveal card 2, Go deeper). Plausible and in line with the gap model, but no approved source. Cut the sentence if review prefers.
3. **"Nicotine is highly addictive"** (Step 1 ch. 2). Covered in spirit by Benowitz 2010 (dependence); confirm reviewers are happy with this wording without a number.
4. **"Fear of quitting is partly produced by the addiction"** (Step 5 ch. 3). This is a method claim (bible §1), not a sourced finding. It is phrased as "a lot of it comes from", not as a fact with a figure; reviewer to confirm the wording is acceptable.
5. **Myth 5, vaping and pouches: "the risks are likely to be different."** RCP 2016 supports "most harm comes from smoke"; check the clinical reviewer is comfortable with this neutral line for pouches, which RCP 2016 does not cover directly.
6. **Myth 4 variant** says "there's less data on vaping/pouches so far". Reasonable, but unsourced; keep only if review agrees, else drop the variant sentence and use the smoking ending for all.
