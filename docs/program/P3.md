### Script (Eleven v4)

Voice direction: Warm and unhurried, quietly curious, like someone who finds this genuinely interesting; a little lighter than P1 and P2, never preachy.

## Chapter 1 · Where we are (0:00)

[warmly] Welcome back. This is day three.

So far you've done two things. You've started to notice the gap: that restless, slightly on-edge feeling that builds between doses. And yesterday you looked at the autopilot, and maybe tried a small wait before your first one of the morning.

However that went, it counts. If you waited fifteen minutes, you learned something. If you didn't wait at all, you learned something too. Nothing this week is a test.

[pause]

Today is a little different. It isn't about waiting, or cutting down. You'll keep using exactly as you normally do. What changes is how you look. Today is about curiosity.

## Chapter 2 · What attention does (0:50)

Some years ago, a research team ran a study with people who smoked and wanted to stop. One group joined a well-established quit program, the standard kind. The other group learned something simpler. They practiced paying close attention, to their cravings and to smoking itself. Not fighting it. Not feeling bad about it. Just noticing, with real curiosity, what was actually going on.

The group that learned to pay attention did better than the group on the standard program.

[pause]

Why would that work? Here's how I think about it.

Your brain keeps a kind of summary of nicotine. It wrote that summary a long time ago, and it rarely checks it. The summary says something like: this helps, this feels good, I need this. And because most uses happen on autopilot, with your attention on your phone, or a conversation, or the next thing on your list, the summary almost never gets tested.

So you end up reaching for a memory of how it feels, more than for how it actually feels today.

Attention is how the summary gets checked. When you really look, your brain gets fresh information. And fresh information is hard to argue with.

## Chapter 3 · The story and the experience (2:15)

[calmly] Let's try something. Think of the one you'd call your favorite. For a lot of people it's the first one with a morning coffee, or the one after a long stretch of work.

Now notice what your mind says about it. Maybe, that one's really good. That one's my moment. That's the story. A story is made of beliefs, and beliefs are made of words.

[pause]

The experience is something else. The experience is what's actually there, moment by moment. {s: The taste of the smoke on your tongue. The heat at the back of your throat. | v: The taste of the vapor, and how quickly it fades. The scratch at the back of your throat. | p: The taste as the pouch goes in. The tingle, or the burn, on your gum.} The small lift in your head. How long that lift really lasts. And what your mind does next, which is often to start thinking about the next one.

Here's what many people find once they look. Most of what feels good is the restlessness stopping. The gap closing. That relief is real, and I'm not going to tell you it isn't. But it's relief from something nicotine set up in the first place.

Someone who doesn't use nicotine doesn't have that gap, so they don't need anything to close it. They aren't holding back from something good. They're simply where you are in the few minutes after you've used, most of the time, without doing anything.

## Chapter 4 · Curiosity, not judgment (3:50)

[gently] Now, an important part. Curiosity is not the same as trying to make it disgusting.

You don't need to hate nicotine to stop. And you don't need to pretend. If you look closely and some part of it feels good, notice that too. Honest beats harsh. The point is to see it as it is. Not as the old story says, and not as some new story says either.

Some thoughts will show up while you look. This is pointless. Leave this one alone, I like it. What if I can't do this. You don't have to argue with any of them. Try putting a few words in front: I'm having the thought that this is pointless.

[pause]

Thoughts are not orders. You can let them talk while you keep looking.

The question to carry with you today is a simple one. What is this actually like, right now? Not what was it like years ago. Not what will life be like without it. Just, what is it like, right now?

## Chapter 5 · Today (5:05)

Here's how today goes. Next, a few cards on the stories most of us tell about nicotine. Then a guided use. I'll talk you through one {s: cigarette | v: vaping session | p: pouch}, start to finish, with your eyes open. You can play it straight away if it's a time you'd use anyway, or save it for later in the day. Please don't add an extra one just for this. Use one you were going to have.

Before you start, you'll say how good you expect it to feel. Afterward, you'll say how good it actually was. If there's a difference, it's worth knowing.

[pause]

You don't have to decide anything today. You don't have to believe anything new. Just look.

[warmly] I'll see you in the cards.

## Step 2 · Reveal · The stories we tell (6 min)

Intro line: "Four things most of us say about nicotine. Tap each one and look at it with fresh eyes."

### Card 1

**Front:** Isn't it the {s: taste | v: flavor | p: taste} I enjoy?

**Answer:** Some of it may be pleasant. But ask yourself: would a nicotine-free version do just as well? If the answer is no, the {s: taste | v: flavor | p: taste} is the wrapping. The nicotine is what you're reaching for.

**Go deeper:** {s: Think back to your very first cigarette. How did it taste then? For many of us, it took a while to get used to. | v: Think about how many flavors you've tried, and how they all start to taste the same after a while. | p: Think about how the flavor fades within minutes, while the pouch stays in.} Over time, the {s: taste | v: flavor | p: taste} got linked with something else: the gap closing soon after. Your brain paired the two, so now the {s: taste | v: flavor | p: taste} itself feels like a promise of relief. That's why it can seem so good when you've waited a while, and so ordinary when you've just had one. Today, try asking two questions at once: what am I tasting, and what am I waiting for? Notice which one matters more to you right now.

### Card 2

**Front:** Isn't it my reward?

**Answer:** The reward is real: a pause, a moment that's yours, a sense that something's done. Nicotine got attached to it. You can keep the moment and choose what goes in it.

**Go deeper:** Look at what you're rewarding yourself with. Usually it's a few things at once: stopping, a change of scene, a moment that belongs to nobody else, a small "well done". Those are worth having. Nicotine came along with them, and it brings something else too: the gap comes back an hour or two later, so each reward quietly sets up the next need. People who don't use still reward themselves, with a coffee outside, a walk, a song, a call to a friend, five minutes doing nothing at all. When you stop, you don't lose the finish line. You get to decide what's waiting there.

Source: Benowitz, 2010

### Card 3

**Front:** Isn't it my break?

**Answer:** Breaks are some of the best minutes in a day: time away, some air, maybe a chat. Nicotine came with the break, but it was never the break itself.

**Go deeper:** A good break does three things. It stops the work, it changes the scene, and it gives you permission to breathe. {s: A smoking break also comes with something people rarely notice: a run of long, deep breaths in and slow breaths out. That breathing is calming on its own, and tomorrow you'll learn a version of it you can use anywhere. | v: A vape break often comes with long, slow breaths out, and that breathing is calming on its own. Tomorrow you'll learn a version of it you can use anywhere. | p: With pouches, there often isn't a break at all. The pouch goes in at your desk and you keep working. So the "break" is mostly the gap closing while nothing else changes. Worth noticing today.} Try this today: on your next break, notice which of those three you're actually enjoying. Then ask whether you'd still want the break if there were no {product} in it. If the answer is yes, the break is the part worth keeping. You're allowed breaks without nicotine. Plan to keep yours.

### Card 4

**Front:** Isn't it just who I am?

**Answer:** You weren't born using. You learned it, and part of you has wanted to stop for a while, which is why you're here. That part is you too.

**Go deeper:** Nicotine can feel woven into who you are: the friends you {s: smoke | v: vape | p: share a tin} with, the way you handle a hard day, even how you see yourself. That's normal after years of repetition. But identity has room for more than one thing. In a large US survey, about two in three adults who smoked said they wanted to quit. Wanting out is common. It may be the most ordinary thing about you. On Day Xero you won't be someone who uses and can't. You'll be someone who doesn't need to. The friends, the humor and the way you look after people all stay.

Source: Babb, 2017. *Variant note: this survey asked people who smoked; for vapes and pouches, keep the sentence as written ("adults who smoked").*

## Step 5 · Myth or fact · Pleasure (6 min)

Intro line: "Five things people believe about the good part. Myth or fact? Go with your gut."

### Statement 1

"Nicotine makes you feel good."

- **Answer:** Partly true
- **Verdict:** Partly. Mostly, it ends a bad feeling it started.
- **Explanation:** Nicotine does act on the brain's reward system and can bring a small lift. But it wears off fast, and regular users feel restless and on edge as it does. A big part of what feels good is that feeling stopping: the gap closing. That's why it often feels best when you've waited longest, and why the lift fades so quickly once the level is back.
- **Source:** Benowitz, 2010

### Statement 2

"People who don't use nicotine are missing out."

- **Answer:** Myth
- **Verdict:** Myth. They don't have a gap to close.
- **Explanation:** Research on mood across the day found that people who smoke tend to feel more tense as each cigarette wears off. Using brings them back to about where non-smokers are most of the time. So the lift is largely a return to normal, and non-users are already there without doing anything. They aren't holding back from something good. They just aren't on the up-and-down.
- **Source:** Parrott, 1999
- *Variant note: this research was in people who smoked. For vapes and pouches, add one line: "The up-and-down between doses comes from nicotine itself." Source: Benowitz, 2010.*

### Statement 3

"The first {s: puff | v: hit | p: few minutes of a pouch} is the best part."

- **Answer:** Fact
- **Verdict:** Fact. That's the moment the gap closes.
- **Explanation:** The first one arrives when your nicotine level is lowest and the gap is widest, so it's the biggest change you'll feel. After that, you're mostly topping up a level that's already back. Your body also gets used to nicotine over the course of a day, which is part of why the first one of the morning tends to feel strongest. If you noticed the good part was early and short, this is why.
- **Source:** Benowitz, 2010

### Statement 4

"I'll never enjoy a party again."

- **Answer:** Myth
- **Verdict:** Myth. The pull fades. The party stays.
- **Explanation:** Early on, a party can bring strong urges, because your brain has learned to link the two. That's worth planning for. But cravings become less frequent over the first weeks after quitting. And on average, people who quit smoking report better mood and quality of life, not worse. The music, the people and the conversation were always the good part. They're still there.
- **Source:** Hughes, 2007; Taylor, 2014

### Statement 5

"Rewards need nicotine."

- **Answer:** Myth
- **Verdict:** Myth. The reward is the moment, not the nicotine.
- **Explanation:** Think about what you're really rewarding yourself with: a pause, some air, the sense that something's done. Those stay. Nicotine adds a hidden cost. About half of it is gone from your body in around two hours, and as it goes the gap comes back, so each reward quietly sets up the next need. Non-users still have treats, breaks and finish lines. You get to choose what goes in yours.
- **Source:** Benowitz, 2010

## Claims to verify

1. **Brewer 2011, study design as described in audio (Step 1, ch. 2).** The script says the mindfulness group "practiced paying close attention, to their cravings and to smoking itself" and compared it with "a well-established quit program" (the American Lung Association's Freedom From Smoking). Check the methods confirm that the mindfulness arm included paying attention while smoking before quit day. If not, cut "and to smoking itself".

Confirm the review covers tolerance developing over the day and resensitization overnight; if it doesn't, cut that sentence and keep the rest.
