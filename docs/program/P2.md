## Chapter 1 · Who decided? (0:00)

[warmly] Good morning. Before anything else, a quick question. [pause]

Yesterday, how many times did you really decide to {s: smoke | v: vape | p: have a pouch}? Not just do it. Decide. A clear moment where you thought, "Yes, I want one now," and then reached for it. [long pause]

If you're like most people, the honest answer is: not many. Most of the time, the {s: cigarette was already lit | v: vape was already in your hand | p: pouch was already in} before any thought arrived. The coffee was poured, you stepped outside, the meal ended, the car started, and there it was. Sometimes you only noticed halfway through. Sometimes you reached for {s: the pack | v: your vape | p: the can} and realized you'd had one just a few minutes before.

That's the autopilot. And it isn't a sign that you're careless or that you don't care. It's a sign that your brain has learned something very well. Today we're going to look at how it got built, and why simply seeing it is the first step to switching it off.

## Chapter 2 · How a cue gets wired (1:35)

Your brain is a brilliant learner. It notices what happens together, and it links them. [pause]

Say you {s: smoke | v: vape | p: have a pouch} with your morning coffee. The coffee comes, the gap closes, you feel settled. The next day, same again. And the day after. Over months and years, that happens hundreds, then thousands, of times. Each time, the link between coffee and nicotine gets a little stronger.

After a while, the coffee doesn't just go with it. The coffee starts asking for it. The smell, the warm cup in your hand, that first sip: they become a signal. Your brain says, "This is the moment the gap closes," and the wanting switches on before you've had a single thought about it.

It's a bit like a song you know well. Someone plays the first line, and the next line arrives in your head on its own. You didn't choose to think of it. It was just there. Or think of the light switch in a home you've moved out of. Years later, walking into a dark room somewhere else, your hand can still reach for the wall where it used to be.

That's what a cue is. Coffee. Finishing a meal. Your phone. The car. A drink. A stressful email. Each one got paired with nicotine so many times that it now pulls on the habit by itself. [pause] It isn't a choice you keep making. It's a groove that's been worn in, one repetition at a time.

## Chapter 3 · Why mornings are so strong (3:20)

[calmly] The first one of the day often feels like the strongest of all. There are two reasons, and they stack on top of each other. [pause]

First, the gap. You've just been asleep for hours without nicotine, so the level is low. You wake up with the gap at its widest, and the first one has the most to close.

Second, the routine. The morning is usually the most repeated stretch of your day. Wake up, phone, coffee, first one. Same order, same place, every single day. It's the most practiced chain of cues you have. Your hands know the moves before you're properly awake.

So the first one of the day isn't proof that you need it most. It's the biggest gap meeting the best-rehearsed routine. Nothing more mysterious than that. [pause] And that makes the morning a very good place to learn.

## Chapter 4 · Seeing it loosens it (4:25)

Here's the hopeful part. The autopilot works best when nobody's watching. [pause]

The moment you notice a cue, something shifts. Instead of coffee leading straight to nicotine, there's a small moment in between. A little space where you can see what's happening. "Ah. There's the coffee cue." [pause]

You don't have to do anything with that space yet. Today, you can still use, exactly as normal. I'm only asking you to see it. When a cue shows up, name it in your head. "That's the after-dinner one." "That's the car." "That's the phone." [pause] Naming it, rather than fighting it, is how you start to step out of the groove. Notice what you feel just before, too. Restless? Bored? Tired? A bit pleased with yourself? You'll map that in a few minutes, and it'll tell you a lot. It's also why you keep using this week. The cues are much easier to see while they're still running.

And here's the part worth holding on to. What was learned can be unlearned. Every link your brain made, it made through repetition. After Day Xero, each time a cue shows up and passes without nicotine, that link gets a little weaker. The cues will still knock for a while. Over the first few weeks, they tend to knock less often, and more quietly.

In a moment, you'll look at which cues run your day, and map your three biggest ones. Then this evening, we'll plan one small experiment for tomorrow morning.

[warmly] For now, keep using as normal. Just catch the cue first.

## Step 2 · Reveal · Your triggers, decoded (6 min)

**Intro line:** "Your biggest cues, one card each. Tap to see what's really going on."

Six cards are written; the app shows the person's top four (see Notes for the build).

### Card 1 · Coffee (`coffee`)

**Front:** Why does coffee feel unfinished without it?

**Answer:** Because they've been paired thousands of times, often first thing, when the gap is widest. Your brain now treats the cup itself as the signal that nicotine is coming.

**Go deeper:** Coffee is usually one of the most repeated pairings there is: same time, same cup, same spot, every day. Each time, nicotine closed the gap while the coffee was in your hand, so the two got wired together. That's why coffee on its own can feel oddly incomplete. The good news is that pairings can be rewired. You don't have to give up coffee. Changing the setting helps: a different mug, a different chair, a short walk with it. So does a plan written as "When I pour my coffee, I'll…". If-then plans like that make follow-through more likely.

*Source: Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006*
*Variant note (smoking):* a line in Step 3 covers caffeine after quitting.

### Card 2 · After meals (`meals`)

**Front:** Why does the one after a meal feel so necessary?

**Answer:** A meal ends, you pause, and it's been a while since the last one. That full, settled feeling and the gap closing arrive together, so they've merged into one.

**Go deeper:** The end of a meal is a natural full stop, and nicotine has filled that full stop for a long time. Because you've usually gone a while without while you ate, the gap is open too. So the relief of closing it lands right on top of the pleasant, full feeling of having eaten, and the two get credited to the {s: cigarette | v: vape | p: pouch}. People who don't use finish meals and feel full and done in exactly the same way. What helps is giving the meal a new full stop of your own: stand up, brush your teeth, make a tea, take a short walk.

### Card 3 · On my phone (`phone`)

**Front:** Why do your phone and {product} go together?

**Answer:** Both fill small, empty moments. Idle hands, idle mind, a scroll and a {s: smoke | v: hit | p: pouch}. They've shown up in the same moments so often that one now cues the other.

**Go deeper:** Phones and nicotine both slot into the in-between bits of the day: waiting, winding down, a minute with nothing to do. {s: A smoke break often comes with a scroll. | v: A vape can be used while scrolling, almost without noticing. | p: A pouch can sit in while you scroll, almost without noticing.} Because both happen so often, the link builds quickly. Watch for the moment you pick up the phone and see if the urge arrives with it. Small changes help a lot: keep the {product} in a different room from where you scroll, or set the phone down for the first minute of a break.

### Card 4 · Driving (`driving`)

**Front:** Why does getting in the car make you reach for it?

**Answer:** Hands busy, mind free, the same route at the same time. The car is a private, repeated space, so it's become one of the strongest places for the habit.

**Go deeper:** Driving is the same few routes, the same times of day and the same seat, over and over. That makes it a perfect place for a cue to form. The car can also feel like your own space, where nobody minds what you do. When you notice the pull as you start the engine, name it: "That's the car." To help on and after Day Xero, give your hands and mouth something else: water, gum, a podcast you save only for driving. And take {product_plural} and anything that goes with them out of the car before your Quit Day.

### Card 5 · Drinking (`alcohol`)

**Front:** Why does a drink make it so much harder to say no?

**Answer:** Drinks and nicotine are often paired in social, relaxed moments, and after a drink or two, plans can feel easier to bend. So two pulls arrive at once.

**Go deeper:** For many people, a drink and a {s: cigarette | v: vape | p: pouch} go together in the same settings: friends, evenings, celebrations. That's a strong, well-practiced pairing. On top of that, alcohol can loosen the part of you that plans ahead, so "just one" can sound more reasonable after a couple of drinks. None of this means you can never drink again. It means the first few weeks after Day Xero are worth planning: fewer drinks, a different setting, a friend who knows, an exit time. Tonight, just notice how often the two show up together.

### Card 6 · Stress (`stress`)

**Front:** Why does stress feel like the time you need it most?

**Answer:** Because the gap and stress feel alike: restless, tense, on edge. Using closes the gap, and that can feel like calm, so stress and nicotine get wired together.

**Go deeper:** When the gap opens during a stressful moment, you feel both at once and can't tell them apart. Using closes the gap, the edge drops, and it feels as if the stress eased. Research on people who smoke found that stress tends to rise between cigarettes and fall after one, which fits the idea that smoking relieves withdrawal rather than stress itself. So the cue isn't really "stress". It's "stress plus the gap". Two days from now we'll look at this loop properly, and at what actually helps in a hard moment.

*Source: Parrott, 1999*
*Variant note (vaping, pouches):* the study was on cigarettes; the wording "people who smoke" is kept for all variants.

## Step 3 · Myth or fact · Habits and helpers (6 min)

**Intro line:** "Five beliefs about the moments that come with nicotine. Tap Myth or Fact."

### Statement 1

**Statement:** "Coffee and {product_plural} just go together."

**Answer:** Myth

**Verdict:** They go together because you've paired them.

**Explanation:** It can feel like a natural match, but nothing in coffee needs nicotine. You've had them together so often that your brain now links them, and the cup itself sets off the wanting. People who don't use drink coffee and enjoy it fully. A pairing that was learned can be relearned: a different mug, a different spot, or a short if-then plan for the first cup all help loosen it.

**Source:** Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006

### Statement 2

**Statement:** "{s: Smoking | v: Vaping | p: A pouch} helps me concentrate."

**Answer:** Myth

**Verdict:** It fixes the poor focus the gap causes.

**Explanation:** It feels true because focus often sharpens right after you use. But as nicotine wears off, the gap makes it harder to settle and concentrate. Using brings you back to your normal level, so it feels like a boost. Difficulty concentrating is a known withdrawal symptom, and it tends to ease within two to four weeks of stopping. After that, the dips that come between doses are gone.

**Source:** Benowitz, 2010; Hughes, 2007

### Statement 3

**Statement:** "{s: A cigarette | v: Vaping | p: A pouch} after a meal helps my digestion."

**Answer:** Myth

**Verdict:** The meal is done either way. The gap isn't.

**Explanation:** The after-meal one feels like it settles things, but most of that feeling is the gap closing right as you sit back, full and relaxed. People who don't use digest their meals perfectly well, and feel just as done. If meals feel unfinished for a while after Day Xero, that's the old pairing, not your stomach. Give the meal a new ending of your own: stand up, make a tea, take a short walk.

**Source:** Benowitz, 2010

### Statement 4

**Statement (smoking):** "Coffee will hit harder after I quit."
**Statement (vaping, pouches):** "Coffee will hit harder after I quit {v: vaping | p: pouches}."

**Answer (smoking):** Fact
**Answer (vaping, pouches):** Myth

**Verdict (smoking):** True. Plan to cut your coffee back a bit.
**Verdict (vaping, pouches):** Unlikely. That finding was about smoke.

**Explanation (smoking):** This one is real. Tobacco smoke makes your body clear caffeine faster. When you stop smoking, the same number of cups can leave a lot more caffeine in your blood. That can feel like jitters, poor sleep or restlessness, which is easy to mistake for cravings. Try cutting back or switching some cups to decaf in your first weeks.

**Source (smoking):** Benowitz, Hall & Modin, 1989

**Explanation (vaping, pouches):** The study behind this was about people who stopped smoking. It's the smoke that speeds up how fast the body clears caffeine, so stopping {v: vaping | p: pouches} isn't expected to change your coffee much. What may change is the habit: coffee might feel strange without a {v: vape | p: pouch} for a while. That's the pairing, and it fades as you practice.

**Source (vaping, pouches):** Benowitz, Hall & Modin, 1989 (smokers only; see Claims to verify)

### Statement 5

**Statement:** "You can't break a habit you've had for years."

**Answer:** Myth

**Verdict:** Long habits can change. Millions have done it.

**Explanation:** A long habit can feel like part of who you are, but the links behind it were learned one repetition at a time, and they can weaken the same way. In the US, more than half of adults who have ever smoked have now quit, many after decades. What helps most is seeing the cues, having a plan for each one, and riding out the urges until they fade.

**Source:** Babb et al., 2017; Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006

## Chapter 1 · Why one small wait (0:00)

[softly] Hi. Let's take a few minutes to get ready for tomorrow morning. [pause]

You've chosen a moment, and a short wait after it. Five minutes, or fifteen, before your first {s: cigarette | v: vape | p: pouch} of the day. I want to explain why something this small matters so much.

If you're like a lot of people who use nicotine, you've rarely, if ever, watched an urge pass. Think about it. The urge shows up, and within moments it's dealt with. You close the gap every time, so you never get to see what the urge does on its own. [pause] Tomorrow, you will. And that one sight matters more than it sounds. Once you've seen an urge come and go on its own, even once, it's hard to fully believe again that you have to answer every single one.

This isn't a test of strength, and it isn't the start of quitting. You can still use as normal tomorrow. It's an experiment with one question: what happens to the urge if I don't answer it straight away?

## Chapter 2 · What it might feel like (1:30)

Let me be honest about what you might notice. [pause]

The pull will probably show up right on cue. A restlessness. Maybe a thought like, "Just have it, what's the point?" Maybe your hands will want something to do. It may feel stronger than you expect, or much weaker. Both are useful to know. You might also feel a little silly, standing in the kitchen watching the clock. That's fine too. Curiosity often feels a bit odd at first.

If it rises, notice that it rises. And keep watching. Urges tend to build, peak, and then ease off, a bit like a wave. [pause] You might find it eases sooner than you thought. You might not. Either way, you've learned something about how it moves.

And if you don't make the full time, that's fine. Whatever you manage counts. Two minutes of watching teaches you more than a day of not looking. And if you forget completely, and only notice once the {s: cigarette's lit | v: vape's in your hand | p: pouch is in}, that's the autopilot showing you just how fast it runs. Make a note of it, and try again the next morning.

## Chapter 3 · Rehearse the morning (2:50)

[slowly] Let's walk through it now, so your brain has seen it once already. Close your eyes if you'd like. [pause]

Picture waking up tomorrow. The room. The light. How you feel. [pause]

Now picture your moment arriving. Maybe it's your feet touching the floor. Maybe it's the first coffee in your hand. Maybe it's the commute, or picking up your phone. [long pause]

Notice the pull. And name it, quietly: "There's the morning cue." [pause]

Feel where it shows up in your body. The chest. The hands. The mouth. Just notice it, the way you did yesterday with the gap. [pause]

Now see yourself doing the thing you picked for those minutes. A glass of water. A shower. Stepping outside. Or just letting the feeling be there, without doing anything about it. [long pause]

And then, when the time's up, you can use if you want to. If you do, notice that first one. [pause] What was it like, after waiting?

## Chapter 4 · Tonight (4:10)

For now, let today go. You mapped your autopilot and named your cues. You know more about your habit tonight than you did this morning. [pause]

If your mind starts running ahead to the morning, that's okay. Take one slow breath, a little longer on the way out, and let the plan rest. It'll be there when you wake up.

[warmly] Sleep well. I'll see you in the morning.

## Claims to verify

1. **"More than half of US adults who have ever smoked have now quit"** (Myth 5). From Babb 2017 (quit ratio about 59% in 2015), but the bible lists Babb 2017 only for "68% want to quit; most attempts are unaided". Confirm the figure and approve this use, or replace with "Millions of people have quit" and drop the source.
2. **Cue learning and extinction** (Step 1 ch. 2 and 4; Reveal cards; Myth 1): cues get paired with nicotine through repetition and set off craving; links weaken each time a cue passes without nicotine. Standard learning theory (cue reactivity), but no approved source. Suggested: Carter BL, Tiffany ST. Meta-analysis of cue-reactivity in addiction research. *Addiction* 1999;94(3):327–340. Until approved, nothing on screen carries a number or a citation for it.
3. **Caffeine and vapes or pouches** (Myth 4, vaping and pouches): "it's the smoke that speeds up caffeine clearance, so stopping vaping or pouches isn't expected to change your coffee much." Mechanism is induction of CYP1A2 by compounds in smoke, not nicotine. Suggested source: Zevin S, Benowitz NL. Drug interactions with tobacco smoking. *Clin Pharmacokinet* 1999;36(6):425–438. If not approved, change the v/p verdict to "That finding was about smoking." and keep the rest.
4. **Slower bowels after quitting** (proposed for Myth 3, not on screen): constipation is a commonly listed short-term effect of stopping, and it would make Myth 3 more honest (nicotine does affect the gut). It isn't in Hughes 2007's core list or the health timeline, so it's left off screen. If a source is approved (e.g. smokefree.gov's withdrawal page), add: "Some people find their bowels are a little slower for a few weeks after quitting. Water, fiber and a short walk help."
5. **Alcohol weakens plans** (Reveal card 5): "after a drink or two, plans can feel easier to bend" and "alcohol can loosen the part of you that plans ahead". General knowledge, hedged, no number; no approved source. Reviewer to confirm, or cut the second sentence of the Go deeper.
6. **"Urges tend to build, peak and ease, a bit like a wave"** (Step 5 ch. 2) and **"Over the first few weeks, they tend to knock less often"** (Step 1 ch. 4). The second is covered by Hughes 2007; the first is the core metaphor (bible §2) and is supported in practice by Bowen 2009's urge-surfing, but the typical duration of a single craving has no approved source. No duration is given on screen or in audio today.
7. **Myth 4, smoking: "Tobacco smoke makes your body clear caffeine faster."** Benowitz 1989 reports the rise in caffeine after stopping and attributes it to faster clearance in smokers; confirm reviewers accept the mechanism line on that source (Zevin & Benowitz 1999, above, covers it directly).
