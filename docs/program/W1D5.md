## Chapter 1 · Day five (0:00)

[warmly] Welcome back. Day five.

You've come through the steepest days. And sooner or later, maybe this weekend, life is going to put you somewhere you used to use without thinking. A bar. A party. A long dinner. A break at work with the usual people.

Today we get ready for that. Not so you can avoid your life. So you can go and enjoy it.

## Chapter 2 · The relaxed moments (0:28)

It's easy to assume the dangerous moments are the stressful ones. Often it's the opposite.

Drinking makes a slip more likely for a lot of people, often when you're out, relaxed and having a good time. Your guard is down. {s: Someone's heading outside and holds the door for you. | v: Someone's passing one around the table. | p: Someone slides a tin across the table.} And the autopilot you mapped in the prep week knows this scene by heart.

It isn't that you want it more on a night out. It's that the old cue fires, and you have less attention spare to notice it.

So we do the noticing now, in advance. Planning ahead in a simple if-then way, if this happens, then I'll do that, makes people more likely to follow through.

## Chapter 3 · Rehearse it (1:21)

[calmly] Let's run through it once, in your head. A rehearsal means that when the moment comes, it's the second time, not the first.

Take a breath, in a way that feels comfortable for you. [long pause]

Picture the next place you're likely to be offered one. Who's there. What's in your hand. [long pause]

Now someone turns to you. {s: "Coming out for one?" | v: "Want a hit?" | p: "Want one?"} [pause]

Notice what happens in your body. A little pull, maybe. A flicker of "go on, then." [long pause] Let it be there. It's an old cue. That's all.

And now hear yourself answer. Short, friendly, finished. "No thanks, I've stopped." [long pause]

Notice that nothing bad happens next. The conversation moves on. [long pause]

Say it once more, in your own words this time. [long pause]

That's your no-line. "Maybe later" leaves the door open, and you'll be asked again in an hour. "I've stopped" closes it kindly.

## Chapter 4 · If it happens (2:48)

A few things make a night easier. Decide before you go. Tell one person you're with that you've stopped. Keep a drink in your hand, any drink. And it's fine to leave early. You can always stay longer next time.

And let's be honest about the other possibility. If you do have one, here's what's true. A slip doesn't undo anything. The most useful thing is to get straight back to your plan, right now, not tomorrow. A slip does make the next one more likely, which is why the next hour matters. Log it, step away from where you are, and carry on.

In a moment, you'll write your no-line and pick three moves for your next night out.

[warmly] Go and enjoy your evenings. You get to keep those. I'll see you tomorrow.

## Step 3 · Myth or fact · Out with people (3 min)

### Statement 1
"Drinking makes a slip more likely." · Answer: **Fact** · Verdict: For a lot of people, yes. · Explanation: Alcohol lowers your guard and is tied to some of your oldest cues. In a study that tracked people day by day after quitting smoking, slips were more likely on drinking days. You don't have to stop going out. Going in with a plan is what changes it. · Source: Kahler, Nicotine Tob Res 2010

### Statement 2
"“Maybe later” is the easy way to say no." · Answer: **Myth** · Verdict: It just moves the question. · Explanation: A soft no gets asked again, usually later in the evening when you're more relaxed. A short, friendly "No thanks, I've stopped" ends it the first time, and most people simply move on. · Source: none (practical advice)

### Statement 3
"If I slip on a night out, the whole week is wasted." · Answer: **Myth** · Verdict: Your days stay. · Explanation: A slip doesn't undo anything. Your days, your money kept and every craving you rode out are still yours. It does make the next one more likely, so the useful move is to log it and get straight back to your plan that night, not on Monday. · Source: Kenford, JAMA 1994
