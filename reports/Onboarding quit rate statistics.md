# Borrow method evidence, never invent success rates

DayXero can show roughly twenty evidence-backed statistics in onboarding, but every one of them describes a quitting method or quitting itself, and none describes DayXero. The strongest are Cochrane's high-certainty findings for cigarette smokers: nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) lifts 6-month quit rates from about 15 to about 23 in 100 in trials (RR 1.55), a patch plus a fast-acting form beats a single form (RR 1.27), and medicine plus behavioural support beats brief advice (RR 1.83). For vapers the usable evidence is thin and low-certainty, and for nicotine pouches there is no cessation trial at all, so onboarding statistics must branch by product. A product success rate, a "with DayXero vs without" graph, a personalised success probability, or any "clinically proven" wording is off the table under FTC, Apple and UK ASA/CAP rules, and would also push the app towards medical-device territory. The app's current NRT line ("It raises your odds of quitting by 50 to 60%") needs rewording, and its combination-NRT citation needs updating from the 2019 review (RR 1.25) to the June 2023 update (RR 1.27). **Most source pages (PubMed, PMC, CDC, NHS, WHO, FTC, ASA, competitor sites) were blocked in this research session, so almost every figure below was confirmed only from search extracts of abstracts and official summaries; each must be checked against its linked source before it is shown to users.** This is general research, not legal advice; counsel should review final onboarding, paywall and listing copy for each market.

### Pouches, and facts that hold across products

There is **no randomised cessation trial for nicotine pouches and no population figure for how many pouch users want to quit**; the only adjacent evidence is for traditional smokeless tobacco, where nicotine lozenges gave RR 1.36 (low quality) and varenicline RR 1.34 (moderate quality) in a 2015 Cochrane review ([Cochrane CD004306.pub5](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD004306.pub5/information/hi)). Cochrane's quit-vaping review likewise calls the evidence for combination NRT "inconclusive" ([UEA record](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/98166)). For pouch users, DayXero's honest numeric content is therefore limited to three things. First, money saved, computed from the user's own stated spend, which needs no citation beyond "based on your answers". Second, nicotine pharmacology: plasma half-life averages about two hours ([Benowitz et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2953858)), and only about 15 to 20% of a pouch's labelled nicotine is absorbed ([PMC11150668](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11150668/)). Third, the Hughes 2007 withdrawal time course, labelled as "based on research in people who stopped smoking". The same three work for vapers.

## The numbers to leave out, and why each one fails

The exclusions fall into five groups. The first is **anything that reads as DayXero's own result**. DayXero has no outcome data, the FTC's 2022 Health Products Compliance Guidance expects randomised human trials on the product itself or an essentially equivalent one, and it judges the net impression of a screen, not its footnote ([FTC guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance); [McDermott summary](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/after-almost-25-years-ftc-issues-new-health-products-compliance-guidance/)). That rules out any success rate, any "X times more likely with DayXero", a projected success curve, a "with app vs without" graph in the Noom style, and a personalised "your chance of success" figure computed from quiz answers.

The second group is **other apps' trial results**. The independent evidence on stand-alone apps is weak: Cochrane found RR 1.00 (0.66 to 1.52) at very low certainty ([Cochrane CD006611](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD006611_can-programmes-delivered-mobile-phones-help-people-stop-smoking)); the largest consumer-app trial, Smoke Free, was null on intention to treat at 6.8% vs 7.0% ([JMIR 2024](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e50963)); and a 2023 meta-analysis found OR 1.03 for apps alone ([Guo et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10160935/)). The January 2026 BMJ EBM headline that apps are "nearly 3 times as good" rests on four studies and 1,402 people at low certainty, was seen only in press coverage, and its authors caution it does not mean commercial apps work ([BMJ Group](https://bmjgroup.com/phone-apps-nearly-3-times-as-good-as-no-basic-support-for-quitting-smoking-long-term/)). iCanQuit's 28.2% vs 21.1% belongs to that product, and Smoke Free's 12.7% among downloaders is a self-selected, non-randomised comparison. None of these transfers to DayXero.

The third group is **statistics that are misquoted, folk figures or internally contested**. CDC's 8.8% is a population measure of recent successful cessation, not a per-attempt success rate ([CDC MMWR](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/wr/mm7329a1.htm)). "It takes 30 attempts" is the upper, model-dependent end of a 6.1 to 29.6 range from one cohort ([Chaiton 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27288378/)). "A craving lasts 3 to 5 minutes" has no traceable primary source; official bodies say only "a few minutes" and secondary sites range up to 20 ([Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/nicotine-dependence/in-depth/nicotine-craving/art-20045454)). The "37% reduction" from urge surfing traces to a blog post, and the study itself found no difference in urges ([Bowen & Marlatt 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20025372/)). Among health milestones, the exact 48-hour and 72-hour claims, any lung-function percentage (agencies conflict between 10% and 30%), "heart disease risk halves at one year" and "stroke risk equals a non-smoker's at five years" have all been softened or lack primary support; the carbon monoxide milestone varies between sources by an order of magnitude ([WHO EMRO](https://www.emro.who.int/timeliner/index.html); [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/tobacco/about/benefits-of-quitting.html)).

The fourth group is **true numbers attached to the wrong feature**. The incentive evidence (RR 1.46, high certainty) is about money paid for verified abstinence, so it cannot support streaks, badges or a pledge ([Cochrane CD004307.pub7](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD004307.pub7/information)); no evidence was found for a daily pledge at all. Cochrane found that behavioural relapse-prevention training, which teaches people to recognise and cope with high-risk situations, gave no worthwhile benefit ([Cochrane CD003999.pub6](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10086300)), so trigger identification must not be sold as "prevents relapse". Mindfulness showed no clear effect on quit rates (RR 0.99, low certainty) ([Cochrane CD013696](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD013696.pub2/information/ru)). No trial tests a preparation phase against none, and observational data show unplanned attempts lasting longer ([West & Sohal 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1382540)), so the prep program cannot claim to raise success. NRT preloading (RR 1.25, moderate certainty) is the nearest support, but it sits outside the standard over-the-counter label and should not be recommended by the app ([Cochrane CD013308.pub2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37335995/)). "85 to 95% of lapses become relapse" is old, secondary-cited and demotivating ([Kirchner 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3296289)).

The fifth group is **bad arithmetic and wrong populations**. Risk ratios from different comparisons (1.55, 1.27, 1.83, 1.15) must never be multiplied or stacked, and odds ratios must not sit beside risk ratios as if comparable. The 3 to 5% unaided figure must not be paired with any treatment figure, because trial control arms quit at about 15%. "NRT doubles your chances versus willpower alone", as Nicorette words it ([Nicorette Australia](https://www.nicorette.com.au/get-ready-to-quit-smoking/smoking-facts)), overstates Cochrane's 1.55 and names a comparator the review did not use. Kwit's "can triple" claim for behavioural support plus NRT could not be matched to a source. No cigarette statistic should appear unlabelled to a vape-only or pouch-only user: the 10 to 20 second brain-delivery figure is for smoke, pouches produce no carbon monoxide, and lung, heart and cancer milestones are smoking evidence. National pack-a-day cost averages are unstable and overstate typical spend; personal arithmetic is better.

## Favourable framing that survives FTC, Apple and UK rules

The rules converge on one test: **what a reasonable user takes away from the whole screen**. The FTC guidance covers health apps expressly, requires borrowed research to be relevant to the specific product, and treats vague qualifiers such as "may" or "helps" as inadequate; "clinically proven" and "science-backed" are establishment claims needing clinical proof on the product ([Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2023/2023-03-02-ftc-revises-health-products-compliance-guidance); [FTC guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)). The UK is stricter in writing: CAP advice says products claiming to help people stop smoking are regarded as medicinal (rule 12.11), that no product has been shown to work without effort (rule 12.8), that "x% success rate" claims need rigorous substantiation, and that self-reported consumer data will not suffice ([ASA advice](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/health-smoking-stopping.html); [ASA substantiation advice](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/substantiation.html)). The closest UK analogue for a behavioural service is hypnotherapy, where support claims are tolerated and success rates are not. Apple's guidelines, read first-hand, add that medical apps "may be reviewed with greater scrutiny", that apps "should remind users to check with a doctor", that marketing in a misleading way "is grounds for removal", and that apps must not "trick users into purchasing a subscription under false pretenses" ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Developer reports show reviewers asking for source links inside the app, next to the health statement ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/703502)).

Within those limits, six moves frame the evidence as favourably as it honestly allows. First, **attribute every number to the method and name the form studied**: "In trials, people who used NRT…", "counselling from a trained adviser", "a text-message program". Second, **lead with absolute pairs** such as "23 in 100 vs 15 in 100": they are vivid, they cannot be misread as "60% of users quit", and they satisfy the clear-and-conspicuous standard better than a bare relative figure. Third, **pick the strongest legitimate comparison for each feature**: RR 1.83 for doing medicine and behavioural support together, txt2stop's doubling for scheduled daily support, Lindson-Hawley for a clean stop on Quit Day. Fourth, **state DayXero's role as structure, not cause**: "DayXero is built around these methods. DayXero itself has not been tested in a clinical trial." That disclosure belongs on the same screen as the number, not in a sheet. Fifth, **turn candour into the differentiator**. Competitors' headline rates are inflated relative to their own best evidence, and third-party reviews now routinely restate the caveats ([getburnout.app](https://getburnout.app/articles/comparisons/best-quit-smoking-apps)). An app that says "apps alone are unproven; medicine plus support is well proven; here is how I help you do both" is making a claim nobody can puncture. Sixth, **make the personal numbers the emotional peak**: money, units and hours computed from the user's own answers are true by construction and need no substantiation.

The medical-device boundary constrains wording as much as numbers. In the US, the FDA exercises enforcement discretion for software giving "periodic educational information, reminders, or motivational guidance" to smokers trying to quit ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/mobile-medical-applications)); in the EU, qualification is driven by the intended purpose expressed in promotional material ([GMED on MDCG 2019-11 rev.1](https://lne-gmed.com/news/revision-mdcg-2019-11-qualification-classification-software-mdr-ivdr)); and Germany's quit-smoking apps that make treatment claims are CE-marked prescription apps ([PTAheute](https://www.ptaheute.de/aktuelles/2023/08/11/dauerhafte-di-ga-bei-binge-eating-und-tabakabhaengigkeit)). "Treats nicotine addiction", "therapy", a product-specific "increases your chance of quitting", and NRT product or dose instructions personalised to the user all assert a medical purpose. Describing NRT evidence is educational; telling a user which product and strength to take is not, so "use each one as its pack directs" and "ask a pharmacist" should stay. Since 26 March 2026 Apple requires new Health & Fitness apps distributed in the EEA, UK or US to declare whether they are a regulated medical device ([Apple news](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=nyqbfz1y)); a "No" declaration next to treatment language is an inconsistency Apple and regulators can both use. Next to the paywall, the same logic tightens: a misleading statistic on the screen before a subscription offer is treated as inducing the purchase, so countdown timers, scarcity and post-purchase qualifiers beside health statistics should be avoided, and price, renewal and trial terms belong on the paywall itself ([Covington on the Negative Option Rule](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/03/ftc-launches-new-rulemaking-on-the-negative-option-rule)).

## Competitors' headline rates are inflated; their borrowed claims hold up

Every product success rate examined is more flattering than the strongest design behind it. The reusable material in the category is the borrowed method claim, not the product rate.

Three lessons follow. QuitSure shows three different numbers on three surfaces, which is itself a credibility weakness; DayXero should use one number, one wording and one citation across app, listing, ads and website. No competitor was found showing a method statistic with an on-screen citation inside onboarding, so sourced cards are open ground. And the conversion upside of statistic screens is unproven: no controlled, isolated test was found, and vendor benchmarks range from roughly 3 to 8% for ratings on a paywall to 10 to 30% for social proof "in winning tests" ([Adapty](https://adapty.io/blog/paywall-optimization-tests/); [App Pricing Lab](https://apppricinglab.com/playbook/paywall-optimization)). A low-authority review analysis attributes 37.6% of negative quit-app reviews to billing, not claims ([App Imp](https://appimp.app/markets/quit-smoking-app)). The regulatory downside of an invented rate is therefore large and the measurable upside modest.

## Recommended copy, screen by screen

Each line below carries a short on-screen source caption and should link to a tappable in-app reference list. Lines are written in the app's existing first-person voice. Where a line is cigarette-only, vape and pouch users should see the alternative or no number.

| Screen | Recommended line | On-screen source | Wording to avoid |

## Corrections to claims already in the app

The app's two existing citations are sound in substance but need four changes. The current onboarding line in `/home/user/DAYXERO/DayXero/Onboarding/DetailScreens.swift` reads "It raises your odds of quitting by 50 to 60%. A patch plus a faster form works best." The figure is still the current Cochrane wording: CD000146.pub5 (2018) reports RR 1.55 at high certainty, and no later version surfaced, though the Cochrane version history could not be opened to prove it ([Cochrane CD000146.pub5](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD000146.pub5/information)). The wording, however, has three faults. "Odds" is the wrong term for a risk ratio. "Your" personalises a trial average. And a bare relative figure invites the reading that 50 to 60% of NRT users quit, when the review's own summary gives about 232 vs 150 per 1,000. The replacement in the copy table fixes all three. The setup screen in `/home/user/DAYXERO/DayXero/NRT/NRTSetupView.swift` shows a large "50 to 60%" with "more likely to quit than going without"; the comparator in the review is placebo or no NRT among trial volunteers, so "than people in trials who used a placebo or no NRT" is accurate, and the accompanying evidence bars should depict roughly 15 and 23 per 100.

Second, the combination-NRT source should move from the 2019 review (RR 1.25) to its June 2023 update, CD013308.pub2: **RR 1.27 (95% CI 1.17 to 1.37), 16 studies, 12,169 participants, high certainty** ([PubMed 37335995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37335995/)). That means changing the label in `/home/user/DAYXERO/DayXeroCore/Model/HealthSources.swift` to "Cochrane review of combination NRT, 2023", pointing the URL at the pub2 record, updating the code comments that say "RR 1.25", and changing "Cochrane, 2019" to "Cochrane, 2023" in the lesson sources in `/home/user/DAYXERO/DayXeroCore/Resources/program.json` and in the sources-sheet accessibility label. One trap: the 2023 review also reports RR 1.25 for NRT preloading, a different comparison, so the old number should not be left anywhere it could be confused with it.

Third, "works best" and "What works best" need scope. Combination NRT beats single-form NRT; it is not the best of all treatments. In Cochrane's 2023 network meta-analysis, varenicline (OR 2.33), cytisine (OR 2.21) and nicotine e-cigarettes (OR 2.37) ranked at the top with high certainty, with combination NRT close behind at OR 1.93 ([Cochrane CD015226.pub2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37696529/)). "Among NRT options, a patch plus a faster form did best" is accurate. The app's existing footnote that varenicline or cytisine "also work" is supported by that review and by the WHO guideline; for US users, note that cytisinicline was not FDA-approved as of the sources read ([MGH](https://www.massgeneral.org/news/press-release/trial-shows-cytisinicline-can-help-quit-vaping)).

Fourth, both claims are cigarette evidence. If the NRT screen is shown to vape-only or pouch-only users, it needs the "trials were in people quitting cigarettes" caveat. The lesson text in `program.json` ("50–60% more likely to quit than people using a placebo or nothing. That's a boost, not a guarantee") is already well worded and needs only the absolute figures and the updated source. Two citation errors in the research brief are also worth recording so they do not reach the app: the behavioural-support-as-adjunct review is CD009670, not CD008286, and the 2025 JAMA youth varenicline trial is Evins et al., with Rigotti 2024 being the separate cytisinicline trial.

## What DayXero can measure later, and how those figures must read

The lesson from competitors is that the denominator is the claim. QuitSure's 80.1% counts only completers who answered a survey; Smoke Free's own early study had 8.5% follow-up, where the missing-equals-smoking rate was 1.6% and the responders-only rate 19.3% ([Crane et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30728950/)). DayXero should therefore record, from launch and with consent, the full funnel: people who finished onboarding, set a Quit Day, reached it, and self-reported abstinence at 7 days, 28 days and 6 months, with the response rate at each point, split by product and by NRT use. The primary figure should count everyone who started and treat non-responders as still using, which is the convention in txt2stop and the vaping text trials. Six months is the field's standard; the ASA has ruled that 28 days after treatment is not long enough to show cessation, and that a self-completed questionnaire is not robust enough to prove a success rate ([ASA Sirius ruling](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/sirius-life-clinic-a14-255344.html)). Verification matters too: in txt2stop, 28% of self-described abstainers were still smoking when tested ([Free et al. 2011](https://read.qxmd.com/read/21722952/smoking-cessation-support-delivered-via-mobile-phone-text-messaging-txt2stop-a-single-blind-randomised-trial)).

Wording must put the denominator and method beside the number. The model is: "Of 1,000 people who started, 310 finished the program; 223 of those told us they were not smoking at day 30 (self-reported, no comparison group)." A headline "72% of finishers were smoke-free" with caveats in a footnote fails the net-impression test, and under the FTC's Endorsement Guides an unrepresentative result needs the generally expected result disclosed clearly ([FTC Endorsement Guides](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/P204500%20Guides%20Concerning%20Endors%20and%20Testimonials.pdf)). Engagement comparisons ("people who checked in daily were more likely to stay quit") must be labelled as associations: in iCanQuit, 57% of users stopped within about a week, and longer users quit more, but motivated people both engage more and quit more ([Bricker et al. 2022](https://jmir.org/2022/8/e39208/PDF)). For the UK, self-reported in-app data will probably never support an objective quit-rate claim; that needs biochemical verification or an independent controlled study. A randomised comparison is the only route to a true efficacy claim in any market, and Smoke Free's null result shows such a trial can disappoint.

Before outcome data matures, three kinds of own-data figure are safer: activity counts that claim no outcome (check-ins completed, cravings logged), store ratings, and subjective survey results worded as opinion ("x% of surveyed users said the SOS tool helped in the moment", with sample size and date). These are this report's inferences from the rules above, not tested positions. One further constraint was read first-hand: Apple's guideline 5.1.3 bars using health data "for advertising, marketing, or other use-based data mining purposes other than improving health management, or for the purpose of health research, and then only with permission" ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)), so turning users' quit data into marketing statistics needs explicit consent and legal review. Finally, the stat screens themselves deserve an A/B test against no stat screen, measured on trial-to-paid conversion and refunds as well as trial starts, since no published test isolates their effect.

## Conclusion

The research inverts the usual quit-app playbook. The category's instinct is to lead with a product success rate and bury the method; the evidence says the method is the only thing proven and the app is the unproven part. DayXero's NRT-supportive design happens to sit on the best-evidenced ground in the field, medicine plus behavioural support, so honest attribution costs it little and gives it a position competitors with inflated rates cannot copy. The weakest points are structural: the prep program, the daily pledge, trigger identification and slip handling have no direct trial support, and vape and pouch users have almost no cessation evidence at all. Copy for those features should explain the reasoning rather than cite a number.

Three actions come before anything ships. Someone must open each linked source and confirm the quoted figure, since this session could not; the NRT copy and the 2019 citation need the corrections above; and counsel should rule on the UK position, where even method-attributed quit claims sit close to CAP's medicinal-claim line. After launch, the most valuable asset is a clean intention-to-treat funnel collected with consent, because the first honest own-data sentence DayXero publishes will be worth more than any borrowed statistic.
