## Key question 1: Reconstruct the program as designed

### Takeaway
DayXero is a fixed-date, abrupt-cessation program: a 7-session "Prep" phase during which the user keeps using nicotine as normal, a chosen Quit Day ("Day Xero"), then 7 daily sessions in Week 1 and 3 sessions a week in Weeks 2–4 (24 sessions, 3–6 minutes each, ending at Day 31 with "Graduation"). Cravings are handled by an urge-surfing SOS screen (breathing + the user's reason + a count-up timer); slips are logged with a 3-step debrief and never alter "days clear"; the evening check-in collects mood, slip yes/no, craving count and a note; "Patterns" infers time-of-day peaks and top triggers from logs; NRT is label-only with reminders.

### Inferences
- The protocol is "prepare, then stop in one go": no cutting down of cigarettes/puffs is asked for; the prep missions are awareness and rehearsal (gap, mindful use, sigh-first, surf one craving) rather than reduction.
- Because Day Xero opens only after prep is finished, a lapse in *engagement* during prep silently moves the *quit date*. That is a hidden mechanic that weakens the commitment the hold-to-set gesture is designed to build.
- "Days clear" is a date arithmetic ("floor(12 × e)" cigarettes not smoked, "$7 × e" kept) and does not respond to slips at all; a person who returns to daily use for weeks still sees a rising clear count unless they go to Settings.
- The program's clinical dose (~105 min over ~5 weeks, front-loaded) sits at the low end of the contact time USPSTF associates with effective combined interventions (see KQ2), and ends at Day 31 while milestone and relapse windows run to a year.

### Gaps
- The prototype does not show which variant a dual user (cigarettes + vape) actually gets beyond the line "Smoking program, with notes for vape"; the design doc notes "Which one a dual user like Sam sees isn't defined." for checklists.
- Audio scripts (the spoken core of each session) do not exist yet ("Audio coming soon"); the critique can only assess on-screen beats.
- The SOS "break menu / movement option" promised by W2S1 and W4S1 feedback text is not present on any SOS screen in the lab; unclear whether it is unbuilt or cut.

## Key question 2: Evidence check against what works

### Takeaway
DayXero's core choices (a set quit date with a preparation period, abrupt cessation, NRT encouragement, ACT-style urge handling, non-punitive slip handling, if-then planning) are consistent with Cochrane, NICE, USPSTF and WHO guidance. The weak points are the ones the evidence is loudest about: standalone apps have little demonstrated effect on their own, so the program's value depends on how hard it pushes medication (combination NRT, preloading where labels allow, prescription options for vapers) and sustained contact; and the current NRT copy actively discourages the best-evidenced regimen (combination).

### Cited Findings

**Setting a quit date; abrupt vs gradual**
- NICE NG209 (people aged 12+) tells clinicians to "advise them that stopping smoking in one go is the best approach", with harm reduction offered to those "not ready to stop in one go" — [NICE NG209](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG209)
- Cochrane 2019 (Lindson, Aveyard, Hughes; 22 trials, 9,219 participants): neither abrupt nor gradual quitting was superior for long-term cessation (RR 1.01); structured reduction "was as successful as abrupt quitting" — [Cochrane CD008033](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD008033.pub4/information); [Klemperer & Lindson letter](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6861854/)
- The 2016 Lindson-Hawley non-inferiority RCT found 4-week abstinence higher with abrupt (49%) than gradual (39.2%) cessation; its authors favoured abrupt, Cochrane's pooled estimate does not — [PMC6861854](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6861854/)
- DayXero's "quit on the day you choose" after a 7-day prep is therefore evidence-consistent; the evidence does not demand that users be *blocked* from quitting sooner (the picker disables dates less than a week out) — `screens/onboarding.js`

**NRT: effect size, combination, preloading, duration**
- Cochrane 2018 (Hartmann-Boyce et al., CD000146): 133 studies, 64,640 participants; RR 1.55 (95% CI 1.49–1.61), high-certainty; plain-language "chances of stopping smoking were increased by 50% to 60%"; absolute ~105/1000 vs ~162/1000; no evidence NRT increases heart attack risk — [Cochrane plain-language summary](https://www.cochrane.org/CD000146/TOBACCO_can-nicotine-replacement-therapy-help-me-quit-smoking); [full review](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD000146.pub5/full). **DayXero's "50–60% more likely to quit" and "Cochrane review of 133 trials, 2018" are accurate.**
- Combination NRT (patch + fast-acting form) beats single-form NRT: RR 1.25 (95% CI 1.15–1.36; 14 studies, 11,356 participants), high certainty; no clear difference between NRT types — [Cochrane CD013308 summary](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD013308_which-combinations-stop-smoking-medications-and-e-cigarettes-help-people-quit); [Cochrane 2023 doses/durations review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37335995/)
- Preloading (starting NRT before quit day): RR 1.25 (95% CI 1.08–1.44; 9 studies, 4,395 participants), moderate certainty limited by risk of bias — [Cochrane 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37335995/)
- No clear evidence that longer patch use helps (low certainty) — [Cochrane 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37335995/)
- DayXero's copy cuts against the combination finding: "Using two together? Only if a pharmacist or doctor suggested it." and starts every plan on Quit Day with no pre-quit option — `screens/me.js`; `NRTSchedule.swift`

**Behavioural support and digital delivery**
- USPSTF 2021 (Grade A): behavioural interventions and FDA-approved pharmacotherapy for non-pregnant adults; combining both "raises quit rates more than usual care or brief advice alone, or pharmacotherapy alone"; most effective combination interventions involve ≥4 sessions with 90–300 minutes total contact; pregnant persons: behavioural counselling only (I statement for pharmacotherapy); e-cigarettes: I statement — [USPSTF](https://uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/tobacco-use-in-adults-and-pregnant-women-counseling-and-interventions)
- WHO's first clinical cessation guideline (2 July 2024) recommends behavioural support plus varenicline, NRT, bupropion or cytisine, and positions text messaging, apps and internet programmes as add-ons or self-management aids — [WHO news release](https://www.who.int/news/item/02-07-2024-who-releases-first-ever-clinical-treatment-guideline-for-tobacco-cessation-in-adults); [guideline PDF mirror](https://adha.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/WHO_Tobacco_Cessation_Clinical_Treatment_Guideline_2024.pdf)
- Cochrane 2021 overview (33 reviews, 312 trials): "Evidence of benefit is strongest for the provision of any form of counselling, and guaranteed financial incentives"; phone counselling, text messaging, individual/group counselling also improved 6-month quit rates; support helped with or without medication — [Cochrane CD013229](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD013229.pub2/information); [ASH explainer](https://ash.org.uk/uploads/The-Cochrane-Review-of-behavioural-interventions-for-smoking-cessation-explained.pdf)
- Cochrane 2019 mobile review: automated text messaging improves quit rates vs minimal support (moderate certainty); the app-vs-less-intensive-support comparison was very low certainty — [Cochrane CD006611.pub5](https://cochranelibrary.com/es/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD006611.pub5/information/ms). (A pooled "RR 1.00, 5 studies" figure for apps appeared in a secondary summary but could not be verified against the review in this session.)
- 2023 JMIR meta-analysis (Guo et al.): app-delivered interventions alone did not show higher abstinence; efficacy increased when combined with pharmacotherapy — [PMC10160935](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10160935/)
- 2025 BMJ Evidence-Based Medicine meta-analysis (searches to 15 Aug 2025): "Smartphone apps may be effective for SC compared with no or minimal support" — [BMJ EBM 31(4):242](https://ebm.bmj.com/content/31/4/242)
- Smoke Free app ASSET RCT (JMIR, Nov 2024; 3,143 UK smokers): offering the app produced no detectable increase in quitting; only 25.3% of the offered arm downloaded it; 7-month follow-up 41.9% — [JMIR 2024;26:e50963](https://jmir.org/2024/1/e50963)
- iCanQuit (ACT-based) vs NCI QuitGuide (US guideline-based, avoidance-focused), JAMA Internal Medicine 2020, N=2,415: 30-day abstinence at 12 months 28.2% vs 21.1%, OR 1.49 (95% CI 1.22–1.83); ACT "taught acceptance of smoking triggers" — [Contextual Science summary](https://contextualscience.org/publications/efficacy_smartphone_applications_smoking_cessation); [NAQC](https://www.naquitline.org/news/531843/Efficacy-of-Smartphone-Applications-for-Smoking-Cessation-A-Randomized-Clinical-Trial.-.htm). A secondary analysis found ACT digital support "significantly more effective when combined with pharmacotherapy" — [Fred Hutch](https://www.fredhutch.org/en/news/spotlight/2024/01/phs-bricker-addiction.html)
- QuitBot (ACT conversational agent) pilot vs SmokefreeTXT (JMIR mHealth 2024): 30-day abstinence at 3 months 32% vs 23% (OR 2.18, 95% CI 1.08–4.39); full RCT (n=760 per arm, 12-month follow-up) registered NCT04308759, results not found — [JMIR mHealth 2024;12:e57318](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2024/1/e57318); [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04308759)
- Quit Genius/Pelago (clinician-assisted digital CBT), N=556 vs very brief advice: biochemically verified abstinence significantly different at 26 weeks (p=.03) but not 52 weeks (p=.16); authors call for trials "without clinician support" — [PubMed 35470860](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35470860/); company release claims 52% 4-week quit rate — [Pelago](https://pelagohealth.com/blog/results-from-quit-genius-clinical-trial-reveal-a-52-quit-rate/)
- quitSTART/SmokefreeTXT: no randomised evaluation of the NCI programs themselves surfaced; vendor case study reports "14% quit rate for those who completed the program" without a control — [ICF case study](https://icf.com/next/case-studies/national-cancer-institute-smokefree)

**Lapses and the abstinence violation effect**
- Marlatt & Gordon's relapse-prevention model: relapse is more likely when a lapse produces an AVE "characterized by internal attribution of blame, reduced abstinence self-efficacy, and feelings of guilt"; Kirchner, Shiffman & Wileyto argue the AVE is "a dynamic cascade of responses to a series of lapse events" — [PMC3296289](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3296289); [Curry, Marlatt & Gordon 1987 via Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/doc/1G2-3403100011.html)
- A 2025 BMC Psychiatry trial of a Marlatt-model CBT intervention reduced smoking, high-risk situations and the AVE vs routine treatment — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/9946dd5781cb476aa2067b73192ed8ec)
- DayXero's slip copy maps directly onto AVE prevention: "A slip is one moment, not a verdict.", "“I slipped, so I've failed” → You haven't. Many people who quit for good slipped along the way.", "“I might as well keep going” → That's the thought that turns a slip into a return. Stop here." — `program.json` W1D6, W4S2
- No peer-reviewed study of streak resets in quit apps was found; app-store reviews describe the pain of resets ("After a relapse, it feels horrible to have to hit the 'break your streak' button") and one opinion piece argues resets "add psychological pressure and failure anxiety" — [Quit Vaping reviews](https://splitmetrics.com/apps/quit-vaping/id1479615245); [blog](https://theionproject-site.pages.dev/blog/why-quit-smoking-apps-fail). The founder's own research note says "full resets as a top complaint in competitor reviews" — `docs/DAYXERO_V1_HANDOFF.md` §1

**Urge surfing vs distraction; mindfulness; ACT**
- Bowen & Marlatt 2009 (123 college smokers): the urge-surfing group did not differ on urge ratings but "smoked significantly fewer cigarettes over a 7-day follow-up period"; the technique "may not initially reduce urges to smoke but may change the response to urges" — [PubMed 20025372](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20025372/)
- Urge surfing vs suppression in smokers: both improved smoking outcomes; only mindfulness improved reported dependence and emotional functioning — [Rogojanski et al. 2011 via BSL](https://scalda.mijn.bsl.nl/coping-with-cigarette-cravings-comparison-of-suppression-versus-/651502)
- The only direct urge-surfing-vs-distraction comparison found is an alcohol thesis in which "occupying one's mind with another activity was more effective at reducing craving and distress from craving acutely than a mindfulness urge surfing approach" — [UGA thesis](https://getd.libs.uga.edu/pdfs/murphy_cara_m_201208_ms.pdf). No smoking trial comparing the two was found.
- iCanQuit's ACT (acceptance of triggers) outperformed avoidance-based QuitGuide (above). DayXero's "noisy passengers" bus metaphor, "Notice and name" ("I'm noticing I'm having the thought"), "Drop anchor" and "make room for the feeling" are standard ACT exercises — `program.json`; `screens/calm.js`

**Identity**
- Tombor et al. 2015 (574 recent ex-smokers, England): 80.3% reported a non-smoker identity; after adjustment, non-smoker identity was associated with continued abstinence at 3 months (p=0.032); observational — [PMC4376294](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4376294/). DayXero's Graduation identity statement ("I don't use nicotine") arrives at Day 31 — `program.json` W4S3

**Dependence assessment**
- Time to first use after waking is the strongest single Fagerström item; the e-cigarette adaptation (e-FTCD) replaces "cigarettes" with "e-cigarettes"; FTND-V "showed inadequate psychometric properties", while PROMIS-E is "psychometrically sound" for e-cigarette dependence — [PMC7976930](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7976930); [PMC9245548](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9245548). DayXero asks "How soon after waking do you first use?" and notes "Using within 30 minutes of waking is a strong sign of dependence." — `screens/onboarding.js`; `Enums.swift` (`FirstUse`)

**Financial incentives and money**
- Guaranteed financial incentives are among the strongest-evidence behavioural components (Cochrane 2021 overview, above). DayXero shows money kept and asks "Spend a little of the money you've kept on something small, just for you." (W2S3) — `program.json`

### Inferences
- The clearest evidence-practice gap is NRT regimen. The app accurately cites the Cochrane NRT effect but then (a) discourages the combination that Cochrane rates high-certainty, (b) never mentions pre-quit patch use that labels in some markets permit, and (c) never signposts prescription medicines (varenicline, cytisine, bupropion) that WHO and NICE rank as first-line. None of this requires dosing advice; it requires accurate framing plus "ask a pharmacist/doctor about…".
- The program's total contact (~105 min, ~24 touchpoints) is in the USPSTF range for effective combined interventions only if users actually complete it; the ASSET trial shows the binding constraint for apps is uptake and adherence, not content. Design decisions that cut adherence (prep gating the quit date; a paywall on Quit Day; sessions dropping to 3/week in Week 2) therefore matter more than any individual lesson.
- "Nothing resets" is well aligned with AVE prevention, but the evidence base is about *attribution and self-efficacy after a lapse*, not about counters being frozen forever. A counter that cannot ever be wrong risks becoming meaningless to the user who has fully relapsed, which undermines the honesty that makes the slip flow credible.
- The ACT/urge-surfing core is supported for smoking (iCanQuit; Bowen & Marlatt), but the one direct comparison suggests distraction/activity reduces acute craving distress faster. The program already *teaches* distraction menus (break menu, lifts, movement) yet the SOS screen offers only breathing + reason; adding the menus to SOS as promised would be both evidence-aligned and consistent with the program's own copy.
- Daily sessions in Week 1 then 3/week is a reasonable taper of contact; the gap in evidence is for the Day 31 cliff edge. Relapse continues well past a month (milestones run to 365 days in the code), and the program leaves only SOS and milestones after graduation.

### Gaps
- No RCT or qualitative study of "streak resets" vs "nothing resets" in cessation apps was found; the claim that resets harm retention rests on reviews and the founder's own competitor research.
- The full QuitBot RCT results, and any peer-reviewed RCT of quitSTART/SmokefreeTXT itself, were not found.
- No direct evidence found on daily *evening* reflection/check-ins as a cessation component; the closest literature (EMA, self-monitoring) was not searched within the call budget.
- Implementation-intention ("if-then") trials in smoking (e.g., Armitage) were not retrieved in this session; the if-then plan builder is assessed on general behavioural grounds only.
- NICE NG209's exact wording on combination NRT, e-cigarettes and relapse could not be fetched (nice.org.uk blocked); the "stop in one go" and 12+ NRT wording comes from search excerpts of the guideline page.

## Key question 3: Pouches and vapes

### Takeaway
The product-variant architecture exists (per-product checklists, counters, identity lines, a nicotine-only health timeline with an explicit thin-evidence note), but the clinical substance for vapers and pouch users is thin: baselines depend on self-reported puffs/pods that the literature says are unreliable, the cigarette-equivalent "nicotine load" is unsourced, the pouch taper is an invented schedule, dual users get the smoking program, and the strongest recent evidence for young vapers (text-message support; varenicline) is not reflected.

### Cited Findings
- Onboarding vape baseline: "pods or disposables a week" (stored as puffs/day) or "By daily puffs"; strength "0.6–1.2% · Low" … "5%"; "Vape strength varies up to 5×. This sets your real nicotine load." — `screens/onboarding.js`
- Nicotine-load card: "Total daily nicotine load", "About 9 cigarettes’ worth of nicotine", "~86 vape puffs at 5%", small print "Rough estimates for comparison. Real intake varies with your product and how you use it." Constants: 1.2 mg absorbed per cigarette ("Benowitz & Jacob, 1984. The source link is still to add."), 0.12 mg per puff at 50 mg/ml ("about ten puffs of a 5% vape match one cigarette") — `screens/onboarding.js`; `DayXeroCore/Logic/OnboardingEstimates.swift`
- "Most people underestimate vaping, because a vape never burns out like a cigarette." — unsourced on screen — `screens/onboarding.js`
- Youth/young-adult vaping consumption measures: a 2026 ITC paper states the reliability and validity of current vaping-consumption measures are limited and self-reported puff measures are part of the problem; "per day" measures fit non-daily vaping poorly — [Gomes et al., Tobacco Control 2026 (preprint PDF)](https://davidhammond.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/2026-ITC-Youth-Vaping-consumption-Tob-Cont-Gomes-et-al.pdf)
- Nicotine salts produce more intense puffing than freebase in young adult vapers (about 25% more total puffs in a lab RCT) — [PMC11316233](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/11316233/); ITC youth surveys tie higher-nicotine products to more frequent vaping, stronger urges and perceived addiction — [NAQC summary](https://www.naquitline.org/news/570309/Trends-in-E-cigarette-Brands-Devices-and-the-Nicotine-Profile-of-Products-Used-by-Youth-in-England.htm)
- A self-perceived addiction question predicted daily vaping better than the Penn State index or E-Cigarette Dependence Scale in youth — [NYU IFP abstract](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2021/journal-article-abstracts/6479688/)
- Cochrane "Interventions for quitting vaping" (Jan 2025; 9 studies, ~5,000 participants): text-message interventions may increase vaping cessation in 13–24-year-olds (low certainty); varenicline may help vs minimal support; unknown for 6-month outcomes and other interventions; it is a living review with a later version (pub3) reported — [PubMed 39777614](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39777614/); [Cochrane CD016058](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD016058.pub2/information)
- This is Quitting (daily texts) RCT, JAMA Internal Medicine 2021, 2,588 vapers aged 18–24: 30-day abstinence at 7 months 24.1% vs 18.6%; adolescent RCT (JAMA 2024, 13–17) 37.8% vs 28%; funder/developer conflict noted — [PMC8129897](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8129897); [JAMA 2024](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2822082)
- Varenicline for youth vaping (Evins et al., JAMA, April 2025; 261 participants aged 16–25, with weekly counselling): vaping abstinence 51% vs 14% (placebo) vs 6% (text-only) at 12 weeks; 28% vs 7% vs 4% at 24 weeks; no switching to cigarettes observed — [Mass General Brigham release](https://www.massgeneralbrigham.org/en/about/newsroom/press-releases/smoking-cessation-drug-helps-young-adults-quit-vaping); [news coverage](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20250423/Varenicline-found-to-help-teens-and-young-adults-quit-vaping.aspx)
- Pouches: UK adult pouch use rose from 0.1% to 1% (2020–2025); 7.5% of men aged 16–24 use them; use in recent quit attempts tripled to 6.5%; lead researcher: "We do not currently know if nicotine pouches are effective in helping people quit smoking." — [The Pharmacist (UK)](https://www.thepharmacist.co.uk/news/nicotine-pouch-use-among-young-men-drives-sharp-rise-ahead-of-restrictions/). No evidence on pouch *cessation* interventions surfaced.
- DayXero pouch taper: "Halving from the onboarding strength down to about 1.5 mg, one week a step" (e.g. 6 → 3 → 1.5 mg), ending the day before Quit Day; copy "Pouches aren't NRT, so there's no label for this. It's a plan you set; change any step to suit the strengths you can buy." — `NRTSchedule.swift`; `screens/me.js`
- Variant handling: "Smoking program, with notes for vape" (dual user shows the main product's program); vaping-specific content in `program.json` is confined to checklists, the battery footer, the W2S3 "What have you noticed?" list ("Not reaching for my pocket", "Throat feels better"…), identity lines, and audio variants not yet recorded — `screens/program.js`; `program.json`
- Health timeline for vape/pouch users uses a nicotine-only group (withdrawal start, "Most nicotine has left your body" with cotinine half-life "about every 15 to 20 hours" sourced to CDC NHANES, withdrawal peak, mood, cravings fade) with note "Research on the long-term effects of vaping is still young, so this timeline sticks to what's known."; three "looks" items (sleep, lips/gums, skin) have no source — `health-timeline.json`
- Counter units: "puffs not taken" / "pouches not used"; Today prep card for vapes adds "+ Urge" — `screens/sessions.js`; `screens/today.js`

### Inferences
- Puff counting is the weakest baseline in the app. A "puffs not taken" counter built on an unreliable self-report will be wrong in a way the user can feel, and the cigarette-equivalent load may understate salt-based disposable use (more intense puffing). Pods/disposables per week, time-to-first-vape, and a self-perceived addiction item are better supported anchors.
- For 18–24 vapers, the evidence that is actually positive (TIQ daily texts; varenicline with counselling) points to (a) a higher-cadence messaging layer than "Two small nudges a day" and (b) explicit signposting to a clinician about prescription options. DayXero currently offers neither for vapers; its NRT content is cigarette-derived (gum "4 mg if your first cigarette is within 30 minutes of waking").
- The pouch taper is a reasonable harm-reduction heuristic but has no evidence; it should be presented as a preference ("some people like to step down first") not as a program recommendation, and the app should not imply a specific endpoint (1.5 mg) is clinically meaningful.
- The honest thin-evidence note on the vape/pouch timeline is the right instinct and should be mirrored wherever vape/pouch-specific numbers appear.

### Gaps
- No study validating pouch counts or pouch dependence measures in young adults was found; no trial of any pouch-cessation intervention was found.
- No source was found for "ten puffs of a 5% vape match one cigarette" or for the 1.2 mg/cigarette absorption figure within this session (the code itself says "The source link is still to add.").
- The Nov 2025 update of the Cochrane vaping review (CD016058.pub3) could not be read (cebm.ox.ac.uk blocked); conclusions above are from the Jan 2025 version.

## Key question 4: Safety and claims

### Takeaway
Most health claims are conservative and faithfully attributed (ACS for smoking, smokefree.gov/CDC for nicotine withdrawal), the NRT stat is correct, and the disclaimer language already meets Apple's "remind users to check with a doctor" expectation. The exposures are: six unsourced cosmetic "looks" claims; one onboarding line that contradicts the protocol ("your brain steps down from this dose"); NRT label sources that are retailer pages; no pregnancy branch; an 18+ gate that is a single tap; placeholder youth resources; no escalation path for persistently low mood; and a regulatory posture that depends on keeping "education and support" framing rather than "treatment".

### Cited Findings

**Claims that check out**

### Inferences
- The NRT planner is the feature closest to Apple 1.4.2 territory. It reproduces label step-downs and lets users "Enter each step yourself" with strengths "25, 21, 15, 14, 10 and 7 mg". The repeated "Follow the label… DayXero doesn't give dosing advice" line is the right mitigation; sourcing presets to manufacturer/regulator labels (FDA DailyMed, MHRA/EMC, TGA) rather than pharmacy shops would strengthen both accuracy and the review story.

The health timeline is population-level and attributed, which is the safer form; the "looks" items are the ones most likely to be challenged because they are unattributed.
- Pregnancy is the biggest safety omission for a product that nudges NRT: one onboarding question ("Pregnant or breastfeeding?") can switch the NRT path to "talk to your midwife/doctor first" and suppress the "Set up your NRT" invitation.
- A mood-escalation rule (e.g., "Rough"/"Really struggling" on 3+ consecutive days → surface the helpline block and "talk to a doctor") would convert existing copy into a safety net at near-zero content cost.

### Gaps
- ACS page wording could not be fetched (cancer.org blocked); the exact current ACS brackets for "1 to 2 years" and "10 years" should be re-verified before launch, since ACS has revised its timeline (CO "12 hours" → "a few days"; lung cancer "10 years" → "10 to 15 years" in some versions).
- US OTC NRT labelling restricts sale to 18+ (prescription for minors) — this is widely stated but was not verified against an FDA source in this session.
- No source found on whether app-store reviewers have flagged quit-nicotine apps under 1.4.1/1.4.2.

### Cited Findings
- Quit date + "stop in one go" matches NICE NG209's advice — [NICE NG209](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG209); DayXero: "Quit on your Quit Day." — `screens/onboarding.js`

## Key question 6: Recommendations, prioritised by expected impact on quit success and retention

### Takeaway

### Inferences
- Items 1–5 are cheap (copy, one unlock rule, trial timing) relative to their likely effect, because they act on medication uptake and on adherence, the two levers the trials say matter for apps.
- Items 6–7 protect trust in the counter, which is the product's most visible artefact; "nothing resets" should remain the default experience of a slip while giving the fully relapsed user a dignified way to restart.
- Items 11–14 matter disproportionately for the stated launch audience (18–35 vapers, pouch users), where the current content is a lightly re-skinned smoking program.

### Gaps
- Expected effect sizes for these changes cannot be quantified from the literature; the ranking is by mechanism (medication uptake, adherence, AVE) and by cost, not by measured impact.
- Whether Apple review treats a label-echoing NRT planner as a 1.4.2 "dosage calculator" is unknown; no precedent was found.

### Inferences
- The curriculum is a compact CBT/ACT relapse-prevention course: motivation and commitment (P1), cue-response mapping (P2–P3), arousal regulation (P4), urge surfing (P5), environment and support (P6–P7), expectation management (DX, W1D1, W2S2), defusion (W1D2), implementation intentions (W1D3, W3S1, slip flow), refusal skills (W1D5), AVE prevention (W1D6, W4S2), behavioural activation and substitution (W1D4, W2S1, W4S1), cue exposure framing (W3S2), identity (W4S3). That is a defensible curriculum; the main structural critique is cadence (daily → 3/week → stop at Day 31) and the Quit-Day paywall, not content.
- The AVE rehearsal (W4S2 slip practice) arrives on Day 28, after the highest-risk period; the live slip flow exists from Quit Day, and W1D6 primes it on Day 7. Moving a shortened practice into Prep 6 or 7 ("here's exactly what happens if you slip") would rehearse the skill before it is needed.
- Mission answers are collected but not fed back (peak ratings, gap lengths, sighs-first). Showing "your craving peaks: 8 → 6 → 4 this week" would turn stored data into the self-efficacy evidence the AVE literature says protects against relapse.
- Water is harmless and consistent with substitution advice, but it is the one place a breakable streak exists; if the product principle is "no streak you can lose", the water streak should be reconsidered or kept clearly separate from quitting.
