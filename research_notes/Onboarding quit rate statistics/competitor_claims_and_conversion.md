## 1. What exact numbers does each competitor claim, where, and citing what?

### Takeaway
The category splits into three groups: (a) apps with a headline product success rate (QuitSure 80-95%, Allen Carr 90%/50%, Pelago/Quit Genius 52%, Reframe 91%), (b) public-health or trial-backed programs that state a relative effect (EX Program "up to 40%", NHS "5 times more likely" after 28 days), and (c) tracker apps that make no outcome claim and lean on user counts, ratings and endorsements (Kwit "WHO-validated", "4.5M+ users"). The requested "71% success rate" for QuitSure could not be found anywhere; QuitSure's public figures seen were 80.1%, 81% and (in-app) 95%.

### Inferences
- "71%" is most likely a misremembered or superseded QuitSure figure. The numbers that exist publicly are 55.8%, 64.5%, 80.1%, 81% and 95%, and QuitSure uses different ones on different surfaces (website 80.1%, Play 81%, in-app 95%). That inconsistency is itself a credibility weakness DayXero can avoid by using one number, one wording, one citation everywhere.
- Apps with weak or no product evidence substitute reach and authority signals (user counts, star ratings, "WHO-validated", named frameworks). Kwit's listing contains no outcome number at all.
- The "5 times more likely after 28 days" claim travels freely between brands (NHS, Kwit, NiQuitin, local councils) because it is a property of quitting, not of a product. It is the most reusable stat in the category.

## 2. What evidence sits behind each claim, and has it been criticised or challenged?

### Takeaway
Only EX Program's text-message arm, Quit Genius and Allen Carr's seminars have randomised trials, and in each case the marketing number is more flattering than the trial's primary long-term result. QuitSure's and Reframe's headline rates come from uncontrolled studies or surveys of completers; the best independent synthesis (Cochrane 2019) found no evidence that smartphone apps improve quit rates, and the largest app RCT (Smoke Free, about 3,100 smokers) was null on intention to treat.

**Regulatory position**
- UK ASA ruling (Sirius Life Clinic, hypnotherapy, "93% success"): claim ruled misleading. A self-completed questionnaire was not robust enough to prove a success rate, and 28 days after treatment was not long enough to show permanent cessation; the advertiser was told not to quote a percentage success rate without rigorous substantiation. [SNIPPET] — [ASA ruling A14-255344](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/sirius-life-clinic-a14-255344.html)
- ASA/CAP advice on stop-smoking ads: avoid specific "x% success rate" claims without rigorous substantiation; success rates "should not be calculated from customers using a money-back guarantee"; unqualified "easy" claims are a recurring problem; stop-smoking product claims can be treated as medicinal and need MHRA authorisation. [SNIPPET] — [ASA advice: Health: Smoking (stopping)](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/health-smoking-stopping.html)
- ASA ruling on NicoBloc: cessation claims misleading because they did not make clear that the user's determination was required. [SNIPPET] — [ASA ruling A14-266848](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/health-beauty-innovations-ltd-a14-266848.html)
- No ASA ruling naming Allen Carr or any cessation app was found. Allen Carr's own page says the 90% claim was withdrawn from UK sites voluntarily. — [allencarr.com](https://www.allencarr.com/?p=6579)
- US FTC: no action against a cessation app was found. Product precedents exist: "Smoke Away" marketers were alleged to lack a reasonable basis for efficacy claims and for claims of superiority to FDA-approved aids, with $7.6 million in redress and penalties in a later case. — [FTC complaint PDF](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/x050064smokeawaycomplaint.pdf); [US DOJ](https://justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/florida-companies-ordered-pay-76-million-redress-and-penalties-connection-marketing-smoke)
- A law-firm summary notes the Opioid Addiction Recovery Fraud Prevention Act lets the FTC seek civil penalties for deceptive advertising of substance-use-disorder treatment products or services, which it says includes tobacco addiction; and the FTC's health-app precedent requires scientific evidence for health-benefit claims. Secondary legal commentary, 2017. — [Mintz](https://www.mintz.com/2017/10/10/building-health-app-part-4-avoiding-ftc-enforcement-action/)
- Noom: $62 million class-action settlement (2022; $56 million cash plus $6 million credits) over "risk-free" trials and auto-renewal that was hard to cancel. This concerned billing, not efficacy statistics; no FTC order against Noom was found. — [Kelley Drye](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/noom-to-pay-over-60m-to-cancel-automatic-renewal-suit)

**Academic critiques of the category**
- Cochrane review (Whittaker et al., 2019): five app trials, 3,079 participants, pooled RR 1.00 (95% CI 0.66-1.52), very low certainty; "no evidence that smartphone apps improved the likelihood of smoking cessation". Text messaging was rated moderate certainty. No newer Cochrane update was found. — [Cochrane CD006611](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD006611_can-programmes-delivered-mobile-phones-help-people-stop-smoking); [NIHR Evidence alert](https://evidence.nihr.ac.uk/alert/text-messaging-support-helps-smokers-quit-but-apps-not-yet-shown-to-work)
- Abroms et al. 2013 content analysis of popular iPhone and Android cessation apps: apps "could be improved by better integration with the Clinical Practice Guidelines and other evidence-based practices". — [PMC3836190](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3836190)
- A UCL comparison of free iPhone apps found "little evidence of improvement in the use of evidence-based BCTs between 2012 and 2014". — [UCL Discovery](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1477317/1/comparative%202012%202014.pdf)
- A 2023 JMIR app-store review found "few apps providing evidence-based support for smoking cessation" alongside many irrelevant, non-functioning and short-lived apps. — [JMIR 2023;25:e45183](https://jmir.org/2023/1/e45183/PDF)
- Haskins 2017, Rajani 2021 and Bold 2023 were named in the brief but were not individually confirmed by search; candidate links surfaced without verification are [JMIR mHealth 2017;5(7):e93](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2017/7/e93/PDF) and [Curr Addict Rep 2023, s40429-023-00524-0](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40429-023-00524-0.pdf).

### Inferences
- Every headline number examined is inflated relative to the strongest design available: completers-only (QuitSure), per-protocol downloaders (Smoke Free), 4-week point prevalence instead of 52-week (Quit Genius), "up to" plus program-wide generalisation of one arm in one age group (EX), refund non-claims (Allen Carr).
- QuitSure's 80.1% would likely fail the ASA's stated test: it is a self-completed survey with a 30-day window, which is the same pattern the ASA rejected in the Sirius ruling. That matters if DayXero markets in the UK and matters as a benchmark of what not to copy.
- With no outcome data, DayXero cannot make any product success-rate claim that would survive ASA or FTC substantiation standards. Method-level evidence (see section 3) is the only defensible statistical content.
- An honest framing is available as a differentiator: the independent evidence says apps alone are unproven, while behavioural support plus medication is well supported, which fits DayXero's NRT-supportive positioning.

### Gaps
- Abstracts of the QuitSure, Quit Genius and Graham 2021 papers were not opened (PMC and PubMed blocked); figures above are from search summaries and should be checked against the papers. The response rate of the QuitSure survey (invited vs responded) and author conflicts of interest were not retrieved.
- Whether the West and Stapleton 2008 paper supports "5 times" as worded by the NHS was not checked first-hand.
- No published researcher critique aimed specifically at QuitSure's or Allen Carr's marketing rates was found beyond NICE's comments and the registry note; no journalism piece was found.

## 3. Which claims borrow general method evidence, and how are they worded and cited?

### Takeaway
The safest and most widely used pattern is the borrowed method claim: a relative statement ("double", "triple", "5 times") about a behaviour or treatment class, worded with "can" and anchored to a comparator ("versus willpower alone", "vs placebo"). Footnote markers are standard in pharma copy (Nicorette) and absent in app copy (NHS app, Kwit).

### Cited Findings
- NHS app: "The evidence suggests you are 5 times more likely to quit for good if you stay smoke free for 28 days". Hedge = "the evidence suggests"; no citation on the listing. [FIRST-HAND] — [App Store GB](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/nhs-quit-smoking/id687298065)
- 2026 Stoptober coverage repeats the figure as a Department of Health statement that smokers who reach 28 days are around five times more likely to stay quit. [SNIPPET] — [South West Yorkshire NHS, 1 Oct 2026](https://www.southwestyorkshire.nhs.uk/2026/10/01/stop-smoking-this-stoptober-with-free-local-support/)
- Kwit reuses the same 28-day/one-month five-times stat on its blog, unsourced. [SNIPPET] — [Kwit blog](https://kwit.app/en/blog/posts/kwit-smoke-free-month)
- Kwit science page: behavioural support plus NRT "can triple" chances versus cold turkey. [SNIPPET] — [Kwit science](https://kwit.app/en/science)
- Nicorette: "can double^ your chances of successfully quitting smoking versus willpower alone" (footnote marker on the number itself); US copy uses "vs. placebo"; the lozenge claim is conditional on dose ("When using 9 or more lozenges per day"). [SNIPPET] — [nicorette.com.au](https://www.nicorette.com.au/get-ready-to-quit-smoking/smoking-facts); [Walgreens](https://www.walgreens.com/findcare/service/nicorette); [Haleon](https://www.haleonhealthpartner.com/en-us/respiratory-health/brands/nicotine-replacement-therapy/nicorette-lozenges/nicorette-lozenges-efficacy/)
- Criticism exists even for the NRT "double your chances" wording: the advocacy site WhyQuit calls it misleading (opinion piece, not a study). — [WhyQuit](https://whyquit.com/011214.html)
- QuitSure borrows general failure statistics as a foil ("96% fail", "2% vs 98%", "Doctor's advice -2%", "Self-control -4%") to make its own rate look large; none carried a visible source. [TEARDOWN]; [SNIPPET] — [Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=org.instaquit.app&hl=en_US)
- EX Program presents a trial of one component (text messages, vapers aged 18-24) as "using EX Program can increase odds of quitting by up to 40%". [SNIPPET] — [Truth Initiative](https://truthinitiative.org/exprogram)

### Inferences
- Wording conventions DayXero can adopt: modal verb ("can"), named comparator, a visible footnote or "Source:" line on the same screen, and the population the number applies to. The two common comparators, "willpower alone" and "placebo", are not interchangeable; copy should use whichever the cited study used.
- Patterns to avoid: comparator-free percentages, "up to", presenting a method statistic so that it reads as the app's own result, and foil statistics about other methods without sources.
- The 28-day claim suits a milestone or goal screen ("reach day 28") more than a paywall, which is how the NHS uses it ("Your goal is to reach 28 days smoke free").

### Gaps
- The precise footnote text and cited trials behind Nicorette's "double" claim were not retrieved.
- The current Cochrane effect sizes for NRT and for behavioural support (needed to source a DayXero method claim) were outside this assignment and not collected here.
- No example was found of an app showing a method statistic with an on-screen citation inside onboarding; whether any competitor does this is unknown.

## 4. Onboarding patterns: where stat screens sit, projections, and comparison graphs

### Takeaway
The dominant subscription-app pattern is a long quiz (27 to 150+ screens), with proof screens interleaved after baseline questions, a "calculating your plan" interstitial, a personalised projection graph, then the paywall. Quit-smoking apps follow it at the short end (QuitSure and Kwit both 27 steps, soft paywall, 3-day trial).

### Cited Findings
- QuitSure: 27 onboarding steps, soft paywall after the quiz, 3-day trial, 10-minute countdown timer, daily-cost price framing. [SNIPPET] — [ScreensDesign](https://screensdesign.com/showcase/quitsure-quit-smoking-vaping)
- QuitSure repeats proof inside the Day 1 lesson (testimonials, "100,000+ clients", "95% success", guarantee) directly before an in-lesson paywall with a "cheaper than a dinner for two" comparison. [TEARDOWN]
- Kwit: 27 onboarding steps, soft paywall, three plans with a 3-day trial on annual. [SNIPPET] — [ScreensDesign](https://screensdesign.com/showcase/quit-smoking-for-good-kwit)
- QUITTR (quit-porn app, same genre of quit funnel): more than 35 screens before a hard paywall with no trial (teardown dated Oct 2026). [SNIPPET] — [ASOitis](https://asoitis.com/case-studies/quittr)
- Noom: funnel of up to 113 screens taking 10-15 minutes; a projection graph shows the user's expected trend line against the "yo-yo effect" of restrictive dieting (a "with us vs without" comparison); a short "calculating your plan" screen precedes the email ask; commitment is built before price appears. [SNIPPET] — [RevenueCat teardown](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/web-to-app-onboarding-funnel)
- Cal AI: about 29 screens ending in a hard paywall with a personalised plan and gamified discount; sign-up framed as "Save your progress" just before the paywall. [SNIPPET] — [ASOitis](https://asoitis.com/case-studies/cal-ai)
- Flo: 159 onboarding screens (March 2024), reviews wall around screen 20, account creation after two paywalls. [SNIPPET] — [Botsi teardown](https://www.botsi.com/resources/flows/apps/flo-2024-03)
- Superwall names a "social proof engine" paywall pattern (Flo, YAZIO, Speak) and recommends specific numbers and star ratings over vague claims. Vendor guidance. [SNIPPET] — [Superwall](https://superwall.com/blog/5-paywall-patterns-used-by-million-dollar-apps)
- Botsi: loading screens can double as selling screens (stat shown while "building your plan"). Vendor guidance. [SNIPPET] — [Botsi](https://www.botsi.com/blog/13-onboarding-principles-high-converting-apps)
- Category complaint data: a report on 375 negative reviews across quit-smoking apps says billing issues appear in 11 of 12 apps and account for 37.6% of negative reviews. Low-authority market-report site. [SNIPPET] — [App Imp](https://appimp.app/markets/quit-smoking-app)

### Inferences
- Typical placement sequence: goal and baseline questions, then a reassurance stat ("you're not alone / this works"), more questions, a mid-funnel reviews wall, a loading interstitial with a stat, a personalised projection, then the paywall with star rating and testimonials.
- For DayXero the honest equivalents of a projection are arithmetic from the user's own answers (money saved, quit date, days of prep) rather than a predicted success curve. A "with app vs without" outcome graph implies a product efficacy claim DayXero cannot substantiate.
- Billing, not statistics, is the main source of negative reviews in the category, so trial terms and cancellation clarity probably matter more for trust than stat wording.

### Gaps
- No teardown evidence was found for quit-smoking apps showing "nicotine-free by <date>" projections, money-saved projections in onboarding, or with/without graphs; whether QuitNow, Smoke Free, Quitzilla or Flamy use them is unconfirmed.
- Mobbin, growth.design and Phiture returned nothing specific to this category.
- Screen-level position of stat screens in QuitSure's 27-step quiz (as opposed to Day 1 lessons) was not observed.

## 5. Quantitative evidence on conversion uplift, and on backlash from exaggerated claims

### Takeaway
No properly measured, isolated test of a statistic or social-proof screen on trial-start or paywall conversion was found. Published numbers are vendor benchmarks or bundled redesign case studies, ranging from about 3-8% (ratings on a paywall) to 10-30% (winning social-proof tests), so the uplift should be treated as plausible but unquantified and tested in-house. Evidence of backlash specifically against inflated success rates is thin; documented user anger in this category centres on billing.

### Cited Findings

**Vendor benchmarks and case studies (not independently measured)**
- Adapty (State of Paywalls 2026, 78,000+ paywalls): social proof lifted conversion by 10-30% in winning tests; visual tests win less often than others. Survivorship caveat: "in winning tests" only. [SNIPPET] — [Adapty](https://adapty.io/blog/paywall-optimization-tests/)
- Adapty's CPO cites 10-20% conversion lifts from onboarding optimisation in general, more from an unoptimised baseline. [SNIPPET] — [Adapty](https://adapty.io/blog/mobile-app-onboarding-testing/)
- RevenueCat case study, ASL Bloom: a behavioural redesign that included social-proof screens in onboarding was associated with a 15% increase in trial conversion and a 12% ARPU uplift. Several changes were bundled, so social proof is not isolated. [SNIPPET] — [RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/asl-bloom-applica-case-study)
- A third-party playbook citing Adapty and RevenueCat case studies puts star ratings and review counts on the paywall at roughly 3-8% lift, and paywall copy tests at 5-20% when successful; it also warns these are benchmarks with differing definitions. [SNIPPET] — [App Pricing Lab](https://apppricinglab.com/playbook/paywall-optimization)
- RevenueCat on Lose It!: longer onboarding raised trial start rates by double digits; the test did not isolate social proof. [SNIPPET] — [RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/why-your-onboarding-experience-might-be-too-short)
- A secondary write-up of a Botsi / Retention.blog case says a paywall redesign with testimonials, a founding story, user photos and an FAQ doubled trial start rate; no control design or sample size given. [SNIPPET] — [dev.to summary](https://dev.to/paywallpro/subscription-onboarding-15-patterns-you-must-know-4n4f)
- Cal AI funnel numbers relayed from a Superwall case study via a social post: 87% of new users see a paywall, 57% start checkout, 63% of those finish. Third-hand. [SNIPPET] — [X post](https://x.com/tobiadonadon_/status/2112602320529764637)
- Superwall publishes guidance on avoiding false positives in paywall A/B tests (title: "How to A/B Test a Paywall (Without Wasting Three Months on a False Positive)"). [SNIPPET] — [Superwall](https://superwall.com/blog/how-to-ab-test-a-paywall)
- Airbridge recommends matching testimonials to the goal the user chose in onboarding and measuring trial start rate; a recommendation, not a result. [SNIPPET] — [Airbridge](https://www.airbridge.io/en/blog/social-proof-for-apps)

**Properly measured results**
- None found. No source reported a controlled test with a stated sample size and confidence interval isolating a statistic or social-proof screen in a subscription app.

**Backlash and trust**
- A Google Play review of QuitSure calls it a "scam app" that is "only here to extort your money"; other reviewers report success. Anecdotal. [SNIPPET] — [Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=org.instaquit.app&hl=en_US)
- Kwit App Store review (15 Jun 2022): "it's NOT cheap to pay for premium; might as well just keep paying for the cigarettes you're trying to quit", and progress features are blocked after premium lapses. [FIRST-HAND] — [App Store US](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/quit-smoking-for-good-kwit/id525441365)
- QuitSure's own blog concedes its 80.1% figure is a snapshot of completers rather than the full picture. [SNIPPET] — [quitsure.app review post](https://www.quitsure.app/post/quitsure-review-2026-does-the-quit-smoking-app-actually-work)
- Third-party reviews now routinely restate the caveat (80.1% is self-reported among users who completed the 6-day program). [SNIPPET] — [getburnout.app comparison](https://getburnout.app/articles/comparisons/best-quit-smoking-apps)
- Billing is the dominant complaint theme (37.6% of negative reviews across 12 quit-smoking apps). Low-authority source. [SNIPPET] — [App Imp](https://appimp.app/markets/quit-smoking-app)

### Inferences
- Expect a single stat or proof screen to be worth single-digit to low-double-digit relative lift at best, with real uncertainty about sign; the 10-30% figure is conditioned on tests that won.
- Because the measurable upside is modest and unproven, and the downside (regulatory exposure, review backlash, comparison articles restating caveats) is asymmetric, sourced method statistics cost DayXero little conversion relative to an invented product rate.
- DayXero should A/B its own stat screen against no stat screen on trial-start rate and on trial-to-paid, since a claim that lifts trial starts but sets false expectations could depress conversion to paid and refunds.

### Gaps
- Reddit (r/stopsmoking, r/QuitVaping) could not be searched or opened; no thread evidence on distrust of app success rates was collected. This is an unanswered question, not a null finding.
- Adapty, RevenueCat and Superwall source pages were not opened, so test counts, win rates and definitions behind the quoted ranges are unverified.
- No evidence was found either way on whether numeric efficacy stats outperform non-numeric proof (ratings, testimonials, endorsements) in health apps.
- No academic study on statistic screens and subscription conversion was found.
