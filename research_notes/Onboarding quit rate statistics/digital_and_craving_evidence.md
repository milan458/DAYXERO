# Digital and psychological quit-smoking interventions: apps, text programs, self-monitoring, craving management (evidence as of November 2026)

Researcher's note on verification (read first): in this session the hosts pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov and www.nhs.uk were not reachable, so no full text or abstract page could be opened. Every number below was read from search-result extracts of the abstract, a Cochrane summary, an institutional repository record or (where flagged) press coverage. Numbers flagged "secondary coverage only" should be checked against the paper before they are shown to users. Where a number was not seen in any search result it is listed under Gaps, not under Cited Findings.

Shorthand: RR = risk ratio; OR = odds ratio; CI = 95% confidence interval; PPA = point-prevalence abstinence; ITT = intention to treat (missing counted as smoking); "verified" = biochemically verified (cotinine or expired CO).

## 1. Cochrane review of mobile-phone interventions (Whittaker 2019, CD006611) and later updates

### Takeaway
The Cochrane review supports automated text messaging (RR 1.54, moderate certainty) but found no detectable benefit of smartphone apps over lower-intensity support (RR 1.00, CI 0.66 to 1.52, very low certainty). No update of CD006611 after 2019 was found; it cannot be cited as showing "quit apps work".

### Cited Findings
- Text messaging vs minimal support: RR 1.54 (CI 1.19 to 2.00); 13 studies, 14,133 participants; I2 = 71%; moderate certainty (downgraded for inconsistency). Outcome is abstinence at 6 months or longer. Population: adult cigarette smokers. — [Cochrane CD006611.pub5](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD006611.pub5); [Cochrane plain-language summary](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD006611_can-programmes-delivered-mobile-phones-help-people-stop-smoking)
- Text messaging added to other cessation support vs that support alone: RR 1.59 (CI 1.09 to 2.33); I2 = 0%; moderate certainty (downgraded for imprecision). — [Cochrane CD006611.pub5](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD006611.pub5)
- Smartphone apps vs lower-intensity support (minimal support or a less intensive app): RR 1.00 (CI 0.66 to 1.52); 5 studies, 3,079 participants; very low certainty. — [Cochrane CD006611.pub5](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD006611.pub5)
- NIHR's summary headline of the review: "text messaging support helps smokers quit but apps not yet shown to work". — [NIHR Evidence alert](https://evidence.nihr.ac.uk/alert/text-messaging-support-helps-smokers-quit-but-apps-not-yet-shown-to-work)
- No 2020-2026 update of CD006611 was found in search; the most recent version located is pub5 (2019). The 2025 Cochrane review that covers text programs is a different review, on quitting vaping (see section 8). — [Cochrane CD016058](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD016058_what-are-effects-treatments-help-people-stop-vaping-nicotine)

### Inferences
- Scope of support: the text-messaging RR supports a claim about automated text programs for cigarette smokers, as a class. It does not transfer to an app. The app RR supports no positive claim at all.
- The 2019 app analysis predates iCanQuit (2020) and most later app trials, so it is out of date for apps; later meta-analyses (section 3) are the better source for the current picture.

### Gaps
- Could not open the Cochrane Library record to confirm whether a newer version exists that search did not index.
- The exact absolute quit rates in the Cochrane summary-of-findings table (assumed control risk and corresponding intervention risk) were not seen.

## 2. Individual app trials

### Takeaway
One large, independently funded trial (iCanQuit) shows an app beating another app (28.2% vs 21.1% at 12 months, self-reported). Most other positive results are small, company-funded, or uncontrolled, and the largest pragmatic trial of a consumer app (Smoke Free, 2024) found no effect in its primary analysis.

### Cited Findings

iCanQuit (ACT-based app) vs QuitGuide (NCI app)
- Bricker et al., JAMA Intern Med 2020;180(11):1472-1480. 2,415 US adult cigarette smokers randomized; follow-up at 3, 6, 12 months. Primary outcome: self-reported 30-day PPA at 12 months: 28.2% (293/1040) vs 21.1% (225/1067); OR 1.49 (CI 1.22 to 1.83); P < .001 (complete-case denominators as reported). Comparator was an active app (NCI QuitGuide, avoidance-based), not no treatment. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7506605); [Fred Hutch release](https://www.fredhutch.org/en/news/releases/2020/09/fred-hutch-led-clinical-trial-shows-new-smartphone-app-helps-smokers-quit.html)
- An outside commentator noted the trial used an older version of QuitGuide, which NCI has since updated. — [GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2020/new-research-fred-hutch-shows-app-can-dramatically-help-smokers-quit/)

Smoke Free (consumer app)
- Jackson et al., J Med Internet Res 2024;26:e50963. Pragmatic RCT, about 3,143 motivated adult smokers; offer of the Smoke Free app plus follow-up vs follow-up only. Primary outcome: self-reported 6-month continuous abstinence assessed at 7 months: 6.8% vs 7.0%; RR 0.97 (CI 0.75 to 1.26), no detectable benefit. Sensitivity analysis restricted to intervention participants who downloaded the app: 12.7% vs 7.0%, RR 1.80 (not a randomized comparison). Two authors are unpaid members of the app's scientific steering group. — [JMIR 2024 e50963](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e50963/XML)
- Crane et al., F1000Research 2018 (exploratory, not pre-registered). 28,112 adult app downloaders who set a quit date, randomized to full vs reduced version. Self-reported 3-month continuous abstinence via in-app questionnaire. Follow-up rate only 8.5% (n=1,213) vs 6.5% (n=901). Missing-equals-smoking: 1.6% vs 0.9%, adjusted OR 1.90 (CI 1.53 to 2.37). Responders only: 19.3% vs 13.8%, adjusted OR 1.50 (CI 1.18 to 1.91). Conflict of interest: the first author originated the app and derives income from it. — [F1000Research 7:1524](https://f1000research.com/articles/7-1524/v2); [PubMed 30728950](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30728950/)

Quit Genius / Pelago (digital CBT with clinician/coach support)
- Webb et al. RCT, 556 UK adults, single-blind, Quit Genius vs very brief advice (VBA). Primary outcome at 4 weeks post quit date: 7-day PPA 44.5% in the Quit Genius arm, verified by expired CO < 10 ppm. — [PMC9597001](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9597001/)
- Long-term secondary outcomes (Nicotine Tob Res 2022, doi 10.1093/ntr/ntac113): self-reported 7-day abstinence at 52 weeks 22.6% vs 13.2%; RR 1.71 (CI 1.17 to 2.50); P = .005. Verified difference significant at 26 weeks (P = .03) but not at 52 weeks (P = .16). Funded by the company that makes Quit Genius. The product includes human clinician support, so it is not an app-only intervention. — [PMC9597001](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9597001/); [PubMed 35470860](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35470860/)

Craving to Quit (mindfulness app)
- Garrison et al., Nicotine Tob Res 2020;22(3):324-331 (NCT02134509). Mindfulness training plus experience sampling vs experience sampling only. Abstinence at 6 months: 9.8% vs 12.1% (overall 11.1%); chi-square(1) = .43, P = .51; no difference. The app appeared to weaken the craving-smoking association without raising quit rates. — [PMC7297096](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7297096); [NAQC summary](https://www.naquitline.org/news/409678/Craving-to-Quit-A-Randomized-Controlled-Trial-of-Smartphone-app-based-Mindfulness-Training-for-Smok.htm)

Pivot (app plus CO sensor, coaching, NRT)
- Pilot RCT, 188 US adults, Pivot vs NCI QuitGuide. Verified abstinence at 12 weeks: 29% (27/94) vs 13% (12/94), ITT P = .008. Verified continuous abstinence at 26 weeks: 21% (20/94) vs 10% (9/94), ITT P = .03. Sponsor: Pivot Health Technologies Inc. (the maker). Intervention includes NRT and human coaching, so it is not an app-only result. — [PMC9732762](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9732762/)
- The "44% quit rate" and "2x at 12 months" claims come from the company's own press release. — [Pivot press release](https://pivot.co/news/pivots-smoking-cessation-quit-rate-reaches-44-in-randomized-controlled-trial-with-significantly-higher-biovalidated-quit-rates-than-control-program-at-one-year)

Clickotine, QuitSure, Kwit (no completed controlled efficacy trial found)
- Clickotine: single-arm study, 416 users; self-reported 7-day abstinence 45.2% (188/416) and 30-day abstinence 26.2% (109/416) at 8 weeks. No control group; authors called for an RCT. — [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Clickotine,-A-Personalized-Smartphone-App-for-Iacoviello-Steinerman/f1ed9d0b3f3fd2d7b79c18cf8008c13352bb0f73)
- QuitSure: 80.1% prolonged abstinence at 30+ days comes from a cross-sectional web survey of 1,286 program completers (JMIR Human Factors 2024), as described on the vendor's own blog. No control group, self-selected completers. — [QuitSure blog](https://www.quitsure.app/post/quit-smoking-apps-compared-success-rates-methods-and-clinical-evidence-2026)
- Kwit: no completed RCT results found; one single-group study of 44 users reported about 30% (n=13) self-reported 7-day abstinence at study end. — [JMIR Serious Games, doi 10.2196/39975](https://doi.org/10.2196/39975)

### Inferences
- How far each result generalizes:
  - iCanQuit: supports "an ACT-based app outperformed a guideline-based app" for that product. It is the strongest single piece of evidence that app content matters, but it is one product, self-reported, and the comparator was another app, so it gives no estimate of app vs nothing.
  - Smoke Free 2024: the closest analogue to a consumer app like DayXero offered to motivated smokers; its primary result was null. The 12.7% vs 7.0% among downloaders is the kind of figure that is easy to misuse, because downloaders are self-selected.
  - Quit Genius and Pivot: bundle human support and/or NRT and were funded by the makers. They do not support claims for a self-guided app.
  - Clickotine, QuitSure, Kwit figures are uncontrolled and should not be used as comparators or as evidence of effect.
- DayXero cannot honestly quote any of these quit rates as its own expected outcome. The defensible wording is of the form "in a trial of a different app, ...".

### Gaps
- Bricker 2020: funding source (believed to be the US National Cancer Institute), retention rates, the ITT missing-as-smoking estimate, and whether any outcome was biochemically verified were not seen in search extracts. The primary outcome as retrieved is self-reported.
- Jackson 2024: exact arm sizes and funding were not seen.
- Webb: the 4-week VBA-arm rate and RR were not seen (only the 44.5% Quit Genius figure).
- No RCT results were found for apps targeting vaping or nicotine pouches specifically, other than text programs (section 8).

## 3. Meta-analyses of cessation apps, 2020-2026

### Takeaway
Pooled results conflict. A 2023 meta-analysis found no effect of apps alone (OR 1.03); a January 2026 meta-analysis reports RR 2.85 for apps vs no/minimal support but from only 4 small studies with low certainty. Neither supports a confident general claim that stand-alone apps raise quit rates.

### Cited Findings
- Barnett et al., Clinical eHealth 2020: 8 studies, 3,543 participants; abstinence at 2 to 6 months vs usual care RR 1.15 (CI 0.85 to 1.57), not significant. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/699d5f770a4c4af28935260081a9a0db)
- Guo et al., J Med Internet Res 2023: 9 RCTs, 12,967 adults (trials published 2018-2022, 6 countries). Apps vs comparators (6 trials): OR 1.03 (CI 0.85 to 1.26), no difference. Apps plus pharmacotherapy vs pharmacotherapy alone (3 trials): OR 1.79 (CI 1.38 to 2.33). — [PMC10160935](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10160935/); [PubMed 37079352](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37079352/)
- Zhou et al., JMIR mHealth 2023: 13 RCTs, 27,240 people aged 30 or under; mobile-phone interventions (mostly text, not only apps) vs inactive control RR 1.51 (CI 1.24 to 1.84). — [PMC10510452](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10510452/)
- Chu et al., BMJ Evidence-Based Medicine, published 13 January 2026 (doi 10.1136/bmjebm-2025-113971): 31 RCTs, 12,802 participants. Secondary coverage only:
  - Apps alone vs no/minimal support, 6-month continuous abstinence: RR 2.85; 4 studies, 1,402 participants; I2 = 0%; low certainty.
  - Apps plus traditional interventions vs traditional alone: RR 1.98; 4 studies, 2,163 participants; I2 = 85.7%; low certainty.
  - Apps plus pharmacotherapy vs pharmacotherapy alone: RR 1.77; 3 studies, 1,502 participants; I2 = 86.0%; low certainty.
  - Psychological-theory-based apps (e.g. ACT, CBT, mindfulness) vs traditional behavioral apps, 7-day PPA: RR 1.69 at 3 months and RR 1.36 at 6 months; reported as high certainty.
  - Authors caution that findings do not mean all commercial apps work, since most have never been trialled. — [HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/healthpro-news/general-health/smartphone-apps-may-boost-smoking-cessation); [BMJ Group press release](https://bmjgroup.com/phone-apps-nearly-3-times-as-good-as-no-basic-support-for-quitting-smoking-long-term/); [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20260116/Smoking-cessation-apps-can-boost-quit-rates-especially-alongside-medication.aspx)

### Inferences
- The most defensible general statement in late 2026: evidence that stand-alone apps increase long-term quitting is low certainty and mixed; apps look more useful alongside medication, and apps built on psychological theory outperform basic ones.
- The Chu 2026 "nearly 3 times" headline rests on 4 studies and 1,402 people; using it as a headline statistic for a different, untested app would overstate the evidence. If used at all, it needs "low-certainty evidence" and "apps that were tested in trials" attached.
- The Chu 2026 theory-based-app finding (RR 1.36 at 6 months) is largely driven by trials such as iCanQuit, so it is not independent of section 2.

### Gaps
- Chu 2026 confidence intervals were not seen in any source; the paper itself was not opened. Do not print these numbers without checking the article.
- No Lancet Digital Health or Addiction meta-analysis specific to cessation apps from 2023-2026 was located.

## 4. Engagement and outcomes (dose-response)

### Takeaway
Within trials, people who use an app longer quit at higher rates, but this is an observational association inside a randomized trial: motivated people both engage more and quit more.

### Cited Findings
- iCanQuit login trajectories (Bricker et al., J Med Internet Res 2022;24(8):e39208; N = 2,415, 182 days of login data): iCanQuit users grouped as "1-week users" 57% (n=610), "4-week users" 28% (n=303), "26-week users" 15% (n=156). Versus 1-week users, 4-week users had OR 1.50 for 30-day PPA at 12 months, and 26-week users had markedly higher odds (reported in a secondary summary as about 397% higher). — [JMIR 2022 e39208](https://jmir.org/2022/8/e39208/PDF); [NAQC summary](https://www.naquitline.org/news/614473/Smoking-Cessation-Smartphone-Application-Use-Over-Time-Do-Usage-Patterns-Predict-12-month-Cessation.htm)
- A 2021 serial mediation analysis of the same trial found the effect of iCanQuit was mediated through engagement and then through increased acceptance of cues to smoke. — [NAQC summary](https://www.naquitline.org/news/587431/Mechanisms-of-Smartphone-Apps-for-Cigarette-Smoking-Cessation-Results-of-a-Serial-Mediation-Model-f.htm)
- Smoke Free 2024: 12.7% abstinence among those who downloaded the app vs 6.8% for everyone offered it and 7.0% in control. — [JMIR 2024 e50963](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e50963/XML)
- English Stop Smoking Services that scheduled more sessions had higher quit rates (service-level association). — [West et al. 2010, UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10224231/)

### Inferences
- A majority (57%) of users of even the best-evidenced app stopped within about a week. That is a useful honesty anchor and an argument for the prep program and daily check-ins, but not proof they work.
- Any onboarding line of the form "people who complete the program are X times more likely to quit" must say it is an association and not proof that completing causes quitting. Reverse causation is also likely: people who relapse stop opening the app.

### Gaps
- Confidence intervals for the trajectory ORs and the exact 26-week OR were not seen.
- No trial was found that randomized people to different engagement levels, which is what a causal dose-response claim would need.

## 5. Craving duration and time course

### Takeaway
No primary study was found that establishes "a craving lasts 3 to 5 minutes". Official sources say only "a few minutes" (NHS) or "brief"; figures of 5-10, 10-20 or 15-20 minutes circulate on secondary sites without a traceable primary measurement. Withdrawal symptoms peak in the first week and last 2 to 4 weeks (Hughes 2007), though that review deliberately excluded craving.

### Cited Findings
- NHS Better Health: "Cravings usually only last a few minutes, so have a distraction ready" (as quoted in search extract; page could not be opened). — [NHS Better Health](https://www.nhs.uk/better-health/quit-smoking/staying-smoke-free/understand-your-smoking-triggers-and-cravings/)
- US National Cancer Institute withdrawal fact sheet: cravings are a common withdrawal symptom; "the urge to smoke will come and go". No duration in minutes given. — [NCI withdrawal fact sheet](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/tobacco/withdrawal-fact-sheet)
- Archived NCI fact sheet (reviewed 2010): cravings "usually last only a very brief period of time"; they "usually begin within an hour or two after you have your last cigarette, peak for several days, and may last several weeks". — [NCI archived fact sheet](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20080916103141/http://www.cancer.gov/cancertopics/factsheet/Tobacco/cravings)
- "Most cravings last 5 to 10 minutes" appears in a Pharmacy Times article that attributes it to Smokefree.gov/CDC; the government page stating it was not located. — [Pharmacy Times / Contemporary Clinic](https://www.contemporaryclinic.com/view/what-should-smokers-expect-when-quitting)
- Mayo Clinic: cravings "often ease up within minutes"; suggests a 10-minute delay. — [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/nicotine-dependence/in-depth/nicotine-craving/art-20045454)
- Other non-primary figures: "10 to 20 minutes" (iCanQuit Australia, NSW government site); "15 to 20 minutes" (Drugs.com). — [iCanQuit NSW](https://www.icanquit.com.au/topics/cravings-triggers-and-routines/how-to-control-smoking-cravings/); [Drugs.com](https://drugs.com/mcd/nicotine-dependence)
- Hughes JR, Nicotine Tob Res 2007;9(3):315-327 (review of 120 studies from 1990-2004): anger, anxiety, depression, difficulty concentrating, impatience, insomnia and restlessness are valid withdrawal symptoms that peak within the first week and last 2-4 weeks. The review excluded craving because its incidence and time course appear to differ. — [PubMed 17365764](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17365764/)
- Laboratory cue-exposure with ratings every 5 minutes for 35 minutes: craving initially decreased in both groups; after 15 minutes the nicotine-gum group had significantly greater reduction. This shows cue-provoked craving declines over minutes without smoking, but does not give a typical episode length. — [J Subst Abuse Treat review](https://www.jsatjournal.com/article/S0740-5472(08)00099-8/pdf)
- EMA evidence on time course after quitting: in a 28-day adolescent study, withdrawal symptoms fell significantly in the week after quit day and by 2 weeks were back to pre-quit levels; craving followed a quadratic (curved) course. — [PubMed 19423695](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19423695/)
- Electronic-diary study of adult quitters (Shiffman group, up to 26 days post-quit): urge intensity after cessation did not generally exceed levels reported during baseline ad lib smoking. — [Life before and after quitting smoking: an electronic diary study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/6918908_Life_before_and_after_quitting_smoking_An_electronic_diary_study)

### Inferences
- Safe wording, supportable by the NHS: "cravings usually pass within a few minutes". "3 to 5 minutes" and "10 to 20 minutes" are folk figures; if a number is wanted, attribute it ("the NHS says a few minutes") and avoid a precise range.
- Safe wording on time course: "withdrawal symptoms are typically worst in the first week and ease over 2 to 4 weeks" (Hughes 2007), with craving episodes becoming less frequent but possibly persisting longer than other symptoms, triggered by cues.
- Evidence here is from cigarette smokers. No equivalent craving-duration data for vaping or nicotine pouches was found.

### Gaps
- No primary study giving a measured median duration of a naturally occurring craving episode was found. Search extracts hinted at a Shiffman finding that, in lapse episodes, smoking occurred within 11 minutes of craving onset in half of episodes, and a review statement that craving duration "could be shorter (5 to 30 minutes)", but neither could be traced to an openable source, so they are not citable as is.
- Heishman's craving work and Shiffman et al. 1997 (decline of urges over days after quitting) were not retrieved.
- The current Smokefree.gov and CDC wording on craving length could not be confirmed.

## 6. Urge surfing, mindfulness, ACT, exercise and breathing

### Takeaway
Mindfulness and urge surfing have supportive small studies but Cochrane found no clear effect on quit rates (RR 0.99 vs matched treatment, low certainty). The best-supported acute craving tool is a short bout of physical activity (large effect on craving in lab studies); breathing evidence is small and short-term. All of this is about momentary craving or small trials, not long-term quitting.

### Cited Findings
- Bowen and Marlatt, Psychol Addict Behav 2009;23(4):666-671: 123 college-student smokers randomized to brief urge-surfing instruction vs no instruction during cue exposure. Groups did not differ on urges; the mindfulness group smoked significantly fewer cigarettes over the 7-day follow-up. Students wanted to cut down; outcome is cigarettes smoked, not quitting. — [PubMed 20025372](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20025372/)
- The widely repeated "37% reduction" for this study comes from a Psychology Today post and was not verified against the paper. — [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-science-willpower/201111/how-mindfulness-makes-the-brain-immune-temptation)
- Brewer et al., Drug Alcohol Depend 2011: 88 nicotine-dependent adults, mindfulness training vs American Lung Association Freedom From Smoking (both in-person group programs). Point-prevalence abstinence 36% vs 15% at end of treatment (P = .063, not significant) and 31% vs 6% at 17-week follow-up (P = .012). — [PMC3541484](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3541484)
- Cochrane, Jackson et al. 2022 (CD013696.pub2): 21 studies, 8,186 participants (mindfulness training, ACT, yoga, distress tolerance). Mindfulness training vs intensity-matched cessation treatment: RR 0.99 (CI 0.67 to 1.46); I2 = 0%; 3 studies, 542 participants; low certainty. ACT vs intensity-matched treatment: not pooled because I2 = 82%. Overall: no clear benefit detected; certainty low or very low. — [Cochrane CD013696.pub2](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD013696.pub2/information/ru); [Cochrane plain-language summary](https://www.cochrane.org/zh-hant/evidence/CD013696_can-mindfulness-help-people-stop-smoking)
- Craving to Quit app trial: no abstinence difference (9.8% vs 12.1%), see section 2. — [PMC7297096](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7297096)
- Physical activity and acute craving, Haasova et al., Addiction 2013 (individual participant data meta-analysis): strength of desire to smoke SMD -1.91 (CI -2.59 to -1.22; 15 studies); desire to smoke SMD -2.03 (CI -2.60 to -1.46; 17 studies). High heterogeneity but all effects in the same direction. Participants were temporarily abstinent smokers in lab studies. — [University of Plymouth record](https://researchportal.plymouth.ac.uk/en/publications/the-acute-effects-of-physical-activity-on-cigarette-cravings-syst-2/)
- Haasova et al., Psychopharmacology 2014 (19 acute RCTs): moderate and vigorous intensity gave the largest craving reductions; no demographic or smoking characteristic moderated the effect. — [University of Plymouth record](https://researchportal.plymouth.ac.uk/en/publications/the-acute-effects-of-physical-activity-on-cigarette-cravings-expl-2/)
- Controlled deep breathing, McClernon et al., Addict Behav 2004;29(4):765-772: within-subject lab study; deep breaths every 30 minutes during 4 hours of abstinence significantly reduced withdrawal symptoms including craving and negative affect vs sitting quietly. Authors call it preliminary. — [PubMed 15135559](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15135559/)
- A 2013 randomized trial found yogic-style breathing reduced craving acutely in the lab, but adherence over the next 24 hours was low and there was no craving reduction at 24 hours. — [PMC7064378](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7064378)

### Inferences
- Supportable for a Craving SOS screen: "short bursts of movement and slow breathing have been shown in lab studies to reduce cravings in the moment". Not supportable: any claim that these techniques raise quit rates.
- Urge surfing: say "in one study, students taught urge surfing smoked fewer cigarettes over the next week" and not that it reduces urges (it did not) or helps people quit (not tested).
- ACT evidence that reaches long-term quitting comes mainly from iCanQuit (section 2) and is product-specific.
- All studies here are in cigarette smokers.

### Gaps
- Roberts et al. 2012 (Psychopharmacology, acute exercise and craving meta-analysis) was not found in search.
- Bricker's WebQuit (web-delivered ACT vs Smokefree.gov) results were not retrieved.
- No study of the "grounding" (5-4-3-2-1) technique for nicotine craving was found.
- Sample size of McClernon 2004 was not seen.

## 7. Self-monitoring, trigger identification and coping planning

### Takeaway
Evidence is indirect: studies link specific behaviour change techniques in stop-smoking service manuals to higher quit rates, but no trial was found that isolates logging cravings, identifying triggers or tracking progress in an app as a cause of quitting.

### Cited Findings
- West, Walia, Hyder, Shahab, Michie, Nicotine Tob Res 2010;12(7) (doi 10.1093/ntr/ntq074): coded treatment manuals of 43 English Stop Smoking Services for behaviour change techniques (BCTs) and correlated them with 4-week quit rates. Some BCTs were associated with higher self-reported and CO-verified 4-week quit rates; a further 5 were associated with CO-verified rates only (e.g. advising on or facilitating social support, providing reassurance). Services scheduling more sessions had higher quit rates. Manuals varied widely and some services had none. Design is cross-sectional and service-level. — [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10224231/); [Maastricht record](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/behavior-change-techniques-used-by-the-english-stop-smoking-servi/)
- Companion paper on group support (West, Evans, Michie 2010): 14 techniques identified in guidance documents, 2 associated with higher short-term self-reported quit rates. — [Maastricht record](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/behavior-change-techniques-used-in-group-based-behavioral-support/)
- Craving to Quit trial: the control arm did experience sampling (repeated in-app self-reporting of craving and smoking) only and had 12.1% abstinence at 6 months vs 9.8% with mindfulness added; there was no no-monitoring arm, so this does not show monitoring works. — [PMC7297096](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7297096)
- Meta-analysis level: apps built on psychological theory outperformed traditional behavioural apps (RR 1.36 at 6 months; secondary coverage). — [HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/healthpro-news/general-health/smartphone-apps-may-boost-smoking-cessation)

### Inferences
- Honest framing: trigger identification, coping planning and self-monitoring are standard components of evidence-based behavioural support (they are in the taxonomy used to describe English stop-smoking services), but their individual contribution has not been isolated.
- Progress, money and health tracking are near-universal in quit apps including those with null trial results (Smoke Free), so their presence is not evidence of effect.

### Gaps
- The full list of BCTs associated with quit rates in West 2010 (believed to include relapse-prevention/coping planning and measuring CO) was not seen; the paper needs to be read before naming specific techniques.
- No RCT or factorial trial isolating craving logging, slip logging, a daily pledge, or savings/health trackers was found.
- No evidence was found either way on whether logging slips helps or harms.

## 8. Daily check-ins, reminders and tailored messages (text programs)

### Takeaway
Automated, scheduled support messages are the best-evidenced digital method: txt2stop doubled verified 6-month abstinence (10.7% vs 4.9%), and a vaping text program raised self-reported abstinence in young adults (24.1% vs 18.6%). These results are for SMS programs, not for app notifications or check-ins.

### Cited Findings
- txt2stop, Free et al., Lancet 2011: single-blind RCT, 5,800 UK adult smokers willing to quit (2,915 intervention, 2,885 control). Intervention: automated motivational and behaviour-change texts; control: texts unrelated to quitting. Primary outcome: biochemically verified (salivary cotinine) continuous abstinence at 6 months: 10.7% vs 4.9%; RR 2.20 (CI 1.80 to 2.68); P < .0001; outcome data for 95% (5,524). Counting all losses as smokers: 9% (268/2911) vs 4% (124/2881), RR 2.14 (CI 1.74 to 2.63). Verification showed 28% of self-described abstainers were still smoking. — [LSHTM repository](https://researchonline.lshtm.ac.uk/id/eprint/303); [QxMD abstract](https://read.qxmd.com/read/21722952/smoking-cessation-support-delivered-via-mobile-phone-text-messaging-txt2stop-a-single-blind-randomised-trial)
- Text2Quit, Abroms et al. 2014: 503 US smokers recruited online, Text2Quit vs self-help material. Biochemically confirmed repeated point-prevalence abstinence at 6 months: 11.1% vs 5.0%; RR 2.22 (CI 1.16 to 4.26). Self-reported: 19.9% vs 10.0%. ITT with losses counted as smokers. — [PMC4545234](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4545234)
- SmokefreeTXT (NCI): no large controlled trial against a no-text control was found. Pilot in homeless smokers (n=50, added to patch and counselling): OR 0.92 (CI 0.30 to 2.84), no difference. Pilot of peer-mentor texts added to SmokefreeTXT (n=200): verified abstinence at 3 months 7.9% (8/101) vs 3.0% (3/99). — [PMC6746087](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6746087); [JMIR mHealth 2019 e13162](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2019/6/e13162)
- This is Quitting (Truth Initiative vaping text program), Graham et al., JAMA Intern Med 2021 (doi 10.1001/jamainternmed.2021.1793): 2,588 US e-cigarette users aged 18-24 (1,304 intervention, 1,284 assessment-only control). Self-reported 30-day PPA at 7 months, ITT with non-responders counted as vaping: 24.1% vs 18.6%. Described as "nearly 40% higher odds". Truth Initiative both runs the program and led the trial. — [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/primary-care/20210519/text-message-intervention-effective-in-reducing-ecigarette-use); [Truth Initiative press release](https://truthinitiative.org/press/press-release/truth-initiatives-quit-vaping-program-effective-helping-18-24-year-olds)
- Cochrane living review, Interventions for quitting vaping (CD016058, January 2025): text-message interventions vs control in 13- to 24-year-olds RR 1.32 (CI 1.19 to 1.47); 2 studies, 4,091 participants; I2 = 0%; low certainty (downgraded for indirectness). A later living update added six studies including an app-plus-text comparison. — [PubMed 39777614](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39777614/); [Oxford CEBM briefing](https://www.cebm.ox.ac.uk/files/reports/iqv-briefing-hcp-and-policy-makers-jan-2025.pdf)
- Pooled: text messaging vs minimal support RR 1.54 (CI 1.19 to 2.00), moderate certainty (section 1). — [Cochrane CD006611.pub5](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD006611.pub5)

### Inferences
- txt2stop is the single most quotable number in this field: large, independent, biochemically verified, 6-month continuous abstinence. It supports "scheduled supportive messages helped smokers quit", for cigarette smokers and SMS. Using it to justify app check-ins or a daily pledge is an analogy, not evidence.
- Absolute rates differ sharply by verification: txt2stop 10.7% verified vs This is Quitting 24.1% self-reported 30-day PPA. These must never be placed side by side as if comparable.
- The vaping text evidence is the only controlled digital evidence found for non-cigarette nicotine, and it is limited to ages 13-24. Nothing was found for nicotine pouches.
- No evidence was found for a "daily pledge" as such.

### Gaps
- The exact OR and CI for Graham 2021 were not seen in any source (the unadjusted OR implied by 24.1% vs 18.6% is about 1.39 by arithmetic, and press material says "nearly 40% higher odds"); check the paper before printing an OR.
- The updated RR in the latest version of the Cochrane vaping living review was not seen.
- No trial was found that tests in-app push notifications or daily check-ins against the same app without them.
