# Baseline quit success and established (non-digital) quit methods: evidence fact sheet (as of November 2026)

> **Method note for the report writer.** Direct page fetches were blocked in this session (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov and www.cdc.gov are outside the session's allowed network domains). Every number below was therefore confirmed from search-result extracts of the abstract / official summary, not from reading the full text. Numbers I could not confirm this way are listed under "Gaps", not under "Cited Findings". Before anything ships in the app, someone should open each cited URL and check the quoted figure against the abstract.
>
> **Scope caveat that applies to almost everything here.** Virtually all of this evidence is from trials and surveys of **adult cigarette smokers**. None of the Cochrane NRT, behavioural, incentive, relapse-prevention or reduction reviews is evidence about quitting vapes or nicotine pouches. See the last section.
>
> **Outcome convention.** Cochrane tobacco reviews measure abstinence at **6 months or longer**, using the strictest definition available (sustained, biochemically verified where possible). RR = risk ratio; OR = odds ratio; CrI = Bayesian credibility interval. ORs are larger than RRs for the same data, so do not put an OR and an RR side by side as if comparable.

## 1. What share of unassisted ("cold turkey") quit attempts succeed, and how many smokers want / try / succeed each year?

### Takeaway
The best-sourced figure is that only about 3-5% of unaided quit attempts last 6-12 months (Hughes 2004), but it is a 2004 review of small, heterogeneous, older studies. US survey data (2022) say 67.7% of adult smokers want to quit, 53.3% try in a year, and 8.8% achieve "recent successful cessation", a population measure that is not a per-attempt success rate.

### Cited Findings
- Hughes, Keely & Naud 2004 (Addiction 99:29-38), systematic review of self-quitters and no-treatment control groups: "3-5% of self-quitters achieve prolonged abstinence for 6-12 months after a given quit attempt". Cigarette smokers only. The authors judged the data too few and too heterogeneous for meta-analysis. — [Hughes 2004, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14678060/)
- Same paper: most relapse among self-quitters occurs within the first 8 days; authors recommend interventions focus on the first week. — [Hughes 2004, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14678060/)
- CDC MMWR, VanFrank et al., "Adult Smoking Cessation — United States, 2022" (published 25 July 2024; MMWR 73:633-641; 2022 National Health Interview Survey; US adults who smoke cigarettes, ~28.8 million): 67.7% wanted to quit; 53.3% made a past-year quit attempt; 8.8% quit ("recent successful cessation"). — [CDC MMWR 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/wr/mm7329a1.htm)
- Same report: among those who tried to quit, 38.3% used treatment (counselling or medication); of smokers who saw a health professional in the past year, 50.5% received advice to quit and 49.2% received assistance. Past-year quit attempts ranged from 74.4% (ages 18-24) to 47.5% (ages 45-64). — [CDC MMWR 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/wr/mm7329a1.htm)
- US Surgeon General's report "Smoking Cessation" (HHS, 23 January 2020): more than three in five US adults who have ever smoked have quit; a majority of cigarette smokers make a quit attempt each year, but fewer than one in ten succeed in quitting each year; fewer than one-third use FDA-approved cessation medication or behavioural counselling. — [Surgeon General 2020, executive summary](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/2020-cessation-sgr-executive-summary.pdf); [key findings fact sheet](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/2020-cessation-sgr-factsheet-key-findings.pdf)
- WHO (2 July 2024): more than 60% of the world's 1.25 billion tobacco users (over 750 million people) want to quit, and 70% lack access to effective cessation services. This covers all tobacco users globally, not just cigarette smokers. — [WHO news release 2024](https://www.who.int/news/item/02-07-2024-who-releases-first-ever-clinical-treatment-guideline-for-tobacco-cessation-in-adults)

### Inferences
- "3-5%" is the citable unaided per-attempt figure, but it should be worded as "roughly 3 to 5 in 100 unaided attempts last 6-12 months" with the 2004 date visible. It is not from a pooled analysis and has no confidence interval.
- The CDC 8.8% is frequently misquoted as "only 8.8% of attempts succeed". It is the share of (current smokers who smoked for 2+ years plus recent quitters) who had quit for at least 6 months in the past year. It mixes aided and unaided quitters and has a different denominator from Hughes.
- Control-arm quit rates in trials are much higher than 3-5% (the Cochrane NRT review assumes about 15% in the control group, see section 3) because trial volunteers are motivated and usually receive some behavioural support. So "unaided 3-5%" and "NRT takes you from 15% to 23%" must not be combined into one comparison.
- A safe, sourced onboarding line: "Most people who smoke want to quit (about 2 in 3), about half try each year, and fewer than 1 in 10 succeed in a given year" (US, CDC 2022 / Surgeon General 2020).

### Gaps
- Could not open the Hughes 2004 full text. Two indexed versions of the abstract describe the evidence base differently (number of self-quitter studies vs no-treatment control groups), so do not quote study counts.
- Did not retrieve the confidence intervals for the CDC 2022 percentages, nor the exact wording of the "recent successful cessation" definition (≥6 months quit within the past year), from the MMWR page itself.
- No comparable, verified UK figure retrieved (Smoking Toolkit Study / NICE NG209 were not reached). NICE NG209 content was not verified in this session at all.
- No equivalent unaided-quit baseline exists in what I found for vapers or pouch users.

## 2. How many attempts does it take on average to quit for good?

### Takeaway
The widely quoted "30 attempts" comes from one Canadian cohort (Chaiton 2016) and is the upper, model-dependent estimate from a range of 6.1 to 29.6 in the same paper; the older "5-7 attempts" figure is an underestimate based on recall by successful quitters.

### Cited Findings
- Chaiton et al. 2016, BMJ Open 6:e011045. 1,277 participants in the Ontario Tobacco Survey (Canada) who had made a quit attempt, followed for up to 3 years from 2005; successful quit defined as abstinence for 1 year. Estimated average number of attempts before quitting successfully: 6.1 under assumptions consistent with prior research; 19.6 using a constant-rate approach; 29.6 using the life-table approach, which accounts for declining success rates across successive observed attempts and which the authors regard as their best set of assumptions. — [Chaiton 2016, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27288378/); [BMJ Open full text](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/6/6/e011045.long)
- The paper's own summary wording is that "it may take 30 or more quit attempts before being successful", and it attributes the lower traditional estimates to reliance on lifetime recall by people who had already quit. — [Chaiton 2016, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27288378/)

### Inferences
- "It takes 30 tries" is weaker than it sounds: a single cohort, cigarette smokers in Ontario, a modelled population average heavily influenced by people who make many attempts, with self-reported attempts. It does not mean an individual needs 30 attempts.
- A defensible onboarding framing: "Most people need several serious attempts. Estimates range from about 6 to as many as 30 (Chaiton 2016)". This supports the app's slip-handling message (a failed attempt is normal, not a verdict) without overclaiming.

### Gaps
- Confidence intervals for the 6.1 / 19.6 / 29.6 estimates were not retrievable from the abstract extracts. A fourth, much higher estimate (about 142, when recalled lifetime attempts before baseline are included) is reported in the paper as I recall it, but I could not verify it in this session; treat as unverified.
- No replication in another country or in vapers / pouch users found.

## 3. Are the app's existing NRT numbers current? (CD000146, CD013308, and the 2023 network meta-analysis CD015226)

### Takeaway
"NRT raises quit rates 50-60%" is still the current Cochrane wording (CD000146.pub5, 2018, RR 1.55, high certainty, no later version found), but it is a relative effect: in absolute terms about 15 to 23 per 100 in trial conditions. The combination-NRT figure should be updated from RR 1.25 (2019 version) to RR 1.27, 95% CI 1.17 to 1.37 (CD013308.pub2, June 2023, high certainty).

### Cited Findings
- **Any NRT vs placebo / no NRT.** Hartmann-Boyce et al. 2018, Cochrane CD000146.pub5. 136 studies identified; 133 studies with 64,640 participants in the primary comparison. RR of abstinence 1.55 (95% CI 1.49 to 1.61) at 6+ months. Rated high-quality evidence. Adults who smoke cigarettes, mostly motivated to quit; clinical and non-clinical settings including over the counter. — [Cochrane CD000146.pub5](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD000146.pub5/information); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29852054/)
- Absolute effect in the same review's summary-of-findings table: 150 per 1,000 quit in control vs 232 per 1,000 with NRT (95% CI 224 to 242), i.e. about 15% vs 23%. The control rate is an assumed/illustrative trial rate, not the unaided real-world rate. — [Cochrane CD000146.pub5 abstract PDF](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD000146.pub5/pdf/CDSR/CD000146/CD000146_abstract.pdf)
- By product, same review: gum RR 1.49 (95% CI 1.40 to 1.60; 56 trials; 22,581 participants); patch RR 1.64 (95% CI 1.53 to 1.75; 51 trials; 25,754 participants). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29852054/)
- Same review: NRT works with or without additional counselling and does not need to be prescribed. — [Cochrane featured review](https://www.cochrane.org/news/featured-review-nicotine-replacement-therapy-versus-control-smoking-cessation)
- **Combination NRT (patch + fast-acting form) vs single form.** Theodoulou et al., Cochrane CD013308.pub2, published 19 June 2023 (update of the 2019 version the app cites). RR 1.27 (95% CI 1.17 to 1.37; I² = 12%; 16 studies; 12,169 participants). High certainty. The review has 68 studies and 43,327 participants in total (2019 version: 63 trials, 41,509). — [Cochrane CD013308.pub2](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD013308.pub2/information); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37335995/)
- Same review: fast-acting NRT and patch give similar long-term quit rates (high certainty, 8 studies); 42/44 mg patches are as effective as 21/22 mg (24-hour) patches and 21 mg outperformed 14 mg in a single study (moderate certainty, imprecision). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37335995/)
- **Cochrane 2023 component network meta-analysis.** Lindson et al., CD015226.pub2 (September 2023). 319 RCTs, 835 study arms, 157,179 participants. Odds ratios vs control (95% CrI): nicotine e-cigarettes OR 2.37 (1.73 to 3.24); varenicline OR 2.33 (2.02 to 2.68); cytisine OR 2.21 (1.66 to 2.97), all high certainty; combination NRT (patch + fast-acting) OR 1.93 (1.61 to 2.34), certainty not formally rated in the abstract. Nicotine patch alone, fast-acting NRT alone and bupropion were also effective with high certainty, but less so than the top group. Non-nicotine e-cigarettes OR 1.16 (0.74 to 1.80), no clear effect; nortriptyline moderate certainty; nicotine tapering low certainty. Low-certainty evidence showed no clear difference in serious adverse events for nicotine e-cigarettes, varenicline, cytisine or NRT. — [Cochrane CD015226.pub2](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD015226.pub2); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37696529/)
- WHO's first clinical treatment guideline for tobacco cessation in adults (2 July 2024) recommends varenicline, NRT, bupropion and cytisine as effective treatments, and says pairing pharmacotherapy with behavioural support increases quit success. — [WHO 2024](https://www.who.int/news/item/02-07-2024-who-releases-first-ever-clinical-treatment-guideline-for-tobacco-cessation-in-adults)

### Inferences
- The app's citation "Cochrane 2018 CD000146" is still the latest version as far as I could tell; the 2023 network meta-analysis is consistent with it and is the more current umbrella source.
- Recommended wording fix: "NRT increases the chance of quitting by about 50-60% (relative)" plus the absolute translation "in trials, about 23 in 100 quit with NRT versus about 15 in 100 without". Showing only the relative figure invites users to read it as "50-60% of NRT users quit", which is false.
- Recommended citation fix: combination NRT RR 1.27 (1.17-1.37), Cochrane 2023 CD013308.pub2, in place of RR 1.25 / 2019.
- Combination NRT (OR 1.93) sits close to varenicline/cytisine/e-cigarettes in the network analysis, which supports an app that encourages patch plus a fast-acting product per label. This is an OR against control and must not be mixed with the RR 1.27 against single-form NRT.
- All of this is about quitting cigarettes. NRT for quitting vaping or pouches is not covered by these reviews.

### Gaps
- I could not open the Cochrane Library version-history page to prove no post-2018 update of CD000146 exists; searches surfaced none.
- The individual component ORs for nicotine patch alone, fast-acting NRT alone and bupropion in CD015226 were not retrievable (my recollection is roughly 1.37, 1.41 and 1.43, unverified; do not quote).
- The exact CI and study count of the 2019 combination-NRT estimate (RR 1.25) the app currently cites were not re-verified.
- NICE NG209 recommendations were not retrieved.

## 4. Behavioural support: adjunct to pharmacotherapy, individual counselling, group therapy, quitlines, and the 2021 overview / network meta-analysis

### Takeaway
Behavioural support works, with high certainty, but effects are modest: counselling OR 1.44 in the 2021 network meta-analysis, and adding or intensifying behavioural support on top of medication gives RR 1.15. Effects look larger (RR 1.57 to 1.88) when the comparator is minimal contact or self-help and no medication is in play.

### Cited Findings
- **Correction to the brief's citation:** the "behavioural support as an adjunct to pharmacotherapy" review is Hartmann-Boyce et al. 2019, **CD009670**, not CD008286. CD008286 is Stead 2016 (combined pharmacotherapy + behavioural support vs minimal care, section 5). — [Cochrane CD009670 summary](https://www.cochrane.org/CD009670/TOBACCO_can-behavioural-support-help-people-stop-smoking); [Cochrane CD008286.pub3](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD008286.pub3/information)
- **Adding / increasing behavioural support for people already using pharmacotherapy.** Hartmann-Boyce et al. 2019 (CD009670): RR 1.15 (95% CI 1.08 to 1.22), 65 studies, 6+ months. Telephone-counselling subgroup: RR 1.25 (95% CI 1.15 to 1.37), 8 studies, 6,670 participants. — [NIHR Evidence alert](https://evidence.nihr.ac.uk/alert/adding-behavioural-support-to-drug-treatment-helps-more-people-quit-smoking); [full text, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6549450)
- **Individual counselling.** Lancaster & Stead 2017 (CD001292.pub3): counselling vs minimal contact, no systematic pharmacotherapy: RR 1.57 (95% CI 1.40 to 1.77), 27 studies, 11,100 participants, high-quality evidence. Where all participants got pharmacotherapy the effect was smaller: RR 1.24. — [Cochrane CD001292.pub3](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD001292.pub3/information)
- **Group behaviour therapy.** Stead, Carroll & Lancaster 2017 (CD001007.pub3): group programme vs self-help programme RR 1.88 (95% CI 1.52 to 2.33; I² = 0%; 13 trials; N = 4,395). Group vs individual counselling of similar intensity: RR 0.99 (95% CI 0.76 to 1.28; 6 trials), no clear difference. — [Cochrane CD001007.pub3](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD001007.pub3/information); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28361497/)
- **Telephone counselling / quitlines.** Matkin, Ordóñez-Mena & Hartmann-Boyce 2019 (CD002850.pub4): 104 studies, 111,653 participants. For people who had called a quitline, additional proactive calls raised quit rates from about 7% to 10% (RR 1.38 in the summary-of-findings table; 72 vs 100 per 1,000). For people who had not called a helpline, telephone counselling raised quit rates from about 11% to 14%. Both moderate certainty. — [Cochrane CD002850.pub4](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD002850.pub4/information); [plain-language summary](https://www.cochrane.org/CD002850)
- **Overview and component network meta-analysis.** Hartmann-Boyce et al. 2021 (CD013229.pub2): overview of 33 Cochrane reviews; 312 RCTs; 250,563 adults who smoked. Counselling OR 1.44 (95% CrI 1.22 to 1.70; 194 studies), high certainty. Guaranteed financial incentives OR 1.46 (95% CrI 1.15 to 1.85), high certainty. Behavioural support increased quit rates at 6+ months with no evidence of harm; effects were somewhat smaller when pharmacotherapy was also provided. Only 4 of the 33 source reviews were rated high confidence on AMSTAR 2. — [Cochrane CD013229.pub2 abstract](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD013229.pub2/abstract/ja); [drugsandalcohol.ie record](https://www.drugsandalcohol.ie/33632/)
- WHO 2024 guideline: recommends brief health-worker counselling plus more intensive individual, group or telephone support, and says digital interventions (text messaging, smartphone apps, internet programmes) can be used as adjuncts or self-management tools. — [WHO 2024](https://www.who.int/news/item/02-07-2024-who-releases-first-ever-clinical-treatment-guideline-for-tobacco-cessation-in-adults)

### Inferences
- These figures are for **human-delivered** support (counsellors, groups, quitlines). They cannot be cited as evidence that an app's check-ins or coaching produce the same effect. DayXero can say "behavioural support alongside NRT adds roughly another 10-20% relative improvement (RR 1.15, Cochrane 2019)" as a fact about counselling, not about itself.
- The RRs are not interchangeable: 1.57 is vs minimal contact with no medication, 1.88 is vs self-help materials, 1.15 is on top of medication, 1.38 is extra calls among quitline callers. The honest summary is "behavioural support helps, a bit less when medication is already in use".
- The WHO sentence on digital tools is the strongest authoritative statement that apps are legitimate adjuncts, but it is a recommendation, not an effect size.

### Gaps
- Total size of CD009670 (I recall 83 studies, about 29,500 participants, and 23,331 in the main analysis, high certainty) and its absolute quit rates were not verified.
- The confidence interval for the quitline RR 1.38 (I recall 1.19 to 1.61) and the comparison behind the RR 1.25 / 41,233-participant row in CD002850 were not verified.
- Other component estimates from CD013229 (text messaging, tailoring, intensity, provider type) were not retrieved.
- The CI for the individual-counselling-plus-pharmacotherapy RR 1.24 was not retrieved.

## 5. Combined pharmacotherapy plus behavioural support versus minimal care

### Takeaway
Medication plus behavioural support versus usual care / brief advice gives RR 1.83 (95% CI 1.68 to 1.98), high-quality evidence. It is the strongest "do both" statistic available, but it dates from 2016 and most trials recruited in healthcare settings.

### Cited Findings
- Stead, Koilpillai, Fanshawe & Lancaster 2016, Cochrane CD008286.pub3: combined pharmacotherapy and behavioural treatment vs usual care, brief advice or less intensive behavioural support: RR 1.83 (95% CI 1.68 to 1.98; I² = 36%; 52 studies; 19,488 participants), 6+ months, high-quality evidence (GRADE). — [Cochrane CD008286.pub3](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD008286.pub3/information); [plain-language summary](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD008286_does-combination-stop-smoking-medication-and-behavioural-support-help-smokers-stop)
- The Lung Health Study was excluded from the pooled estimate and reported separately because its effect was much larger: RR 3.88, 5,887 participants. — [Cochrane CD008286.pub3](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD008286.pub3/information)
- The only effect modifier detected was recruitment setting. — [Cochrane CD008286.pub3](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD008286.pub3/information)

### Inferences
- Usable line: "Combining stop-smoking medication with behavioural support makes quitting about 70-100% more likely than brief advice or usual care (RR 1.83, 95% CI 1.68-1.98; Cochrane 2016)". Relative effect only.
- Do not multiply this with the NRT RR 1.55 or the adjunct RR 1.15; they come from different comparisons and overlap.
- The comparator is "minimal care", not "cold turkey alone", and the pharmacotherapy was mostly NRT but not exclusively.

### Gaps
- Absolute quit rates in the two arms were not retrieved.
- No update since 2016 was found; could not confirm from the Cochrane version history.

## 6. Quit dates, preparation, planning, NRT preloading, and abrupt versus gradual

### Takeaway
There is no good evidence that setting a quit date or doing a preparation phase raises success by itself; observational data even show unplanned attempts lasting longer. What is supported: starting NRT before quit day (preloading, RR 1.25, moderate certainty), and abrupt quitting on the quit day being at least as good as cutting down first (one good RCT favours abrupt; the Cochrane pooled estimate shows no difference).

### Cited Findings
- **NRT preloading** (using NRT before the quit day vs starting on quit day). Theodoulou et al. 2023, Cochrane CD013308.pub2: RR 1.25 (95% CI 1.08 to 1.44; I² = 0%; 9 studies; 4,395 participants). Moderate certainty, downgraded for risk of bias; the result weakened when high-risk-of-bias studies were excluded and the authors call for more research. — [Cochrane CD013308.pub2 abstract PDF](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD013308.pub2/pdf/CDSR/CD013308/CD013308_abstract.pdf); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37335995/)
- **Abrupt vs gradual, RCT.** Lindson-Hawley et al. 2016, Annals of Internal Medicine; 697 adult smokers in England who wanted to quit; non-inferiority trial; both arms had a quit day 2 weeks after enrolment, behavioural support and NRT. Abstinence at 4 weeks: gradual 39.2% (95% CI 34.0% to 44.4%) vs abrupt 49.0% (43.8% to 54.2%), RR 0.80 (0.66 to 0.93). At 6 months: gradual 15.5% (12.0% to 19.7%) vs abrupt 22.0% (18.0% to 26.6%), RR 0.71 (0.46 to 0.91). Gradual was not non-inferior. Fewer in the gradual arm actually made a quit attempt on quit day (61.4% vs 71.0%). Blinding was impossible. — [UCL Discovery record](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1477650); [Oxford summary](https://www.phc.ox.ac.uk/news/if-you-want-to-quit-smoking-do-it-now)
- **Abrupt vs gradual, pooled.** Lindson et al. 2019, Cochrane CD013183.pub2: 22 trials, 9,219 participants; reduction-to-quit vs abrupt quitting RR 1.01; moderate-certainty evidence that neither approach gives superior long-term quit rates. Subgroup signal that reduction-to-quit may do better than abrupt quitting when varenicline is used as the reduction aid. Arms were not always matched for pre- and post-quit NRT. — [Cochrane CD013183.pub2](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD013183.pub2); [co-authored letter, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6861854)
- **Planned vs unplanned attempts (observational).** West & Sohal 2006, BMJ; household survey in England, 918 smokers and 996 ex-smokers. 48.6% of smokers said their most recent quit attempt was made immediately with no prior planning. Among attempts made 6 months to 5 years earlier, 65.4% of unplanned vs 42.3% of planned attempts lasted at least 6 months: OR 2.6 (95% CI 1.9 to 3.6); adjusted for age, sex and socioeconomic group OR 2.0 (1.2 to 3.2). — [West & Sohal 2006, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1382540)
- A later English population study (Garnett, Shahab, Raupach, West, Brown; Nicotine & Tobacco Research 2020) concluded that the higher success of spontaneous attempts appears to be explained by spontaneous quitters being more likely to stop without cutting down first. — [UCL Discovery manuscript](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10078079/7/Brown_Understanding%20the%20association%20between%20spontaneous%20quit%20attempts%20and%20improved%20smoking%20cessation%20success%20rates_AAM.pdf)

### Inferences
- DayXero should **not** claim that a prep programme or a chosen Quit Day is proven to raise quit rates. No trial I found tests "prep phase vs none". The planned-vs-unplanned data are cross-sectional, recall-based and confounded (heavier, more dependent smokers plan more), so they also do not prove planning is harmful.
- The most defensible evidence-based rationale for a prep phase is NRT preloading (RR 1.25, moderate certainty). Caveat: preloading is generally outside the standard OTC label, so it conflicts with "label taper schedules" and needs regulatory/medical review before the app recommends it.
- Coincidence to avoid confusion: preloading RR 1.25 is the same number as the app's old combination-NRT RR 1.25. They are different comparisons.
- For Quit Day design, the evidence supports a clean stop on the day: in Lindson-Hawley both arms had a set quit day 2 weeks out, and abrupt did better. A short prep period ending in an abrupt stop is consistent with the trial; a prep phase built around tapering cigarettes is not supported as superior.
- Contested: Lindson-Hawley 2016 (abrupt better) vs Cochrane 2019 (no difference). The app can truthfully say "stopping all at once is at least as effective as cutting down first".

### Gaps
- The CI for the Cochrane 2019 RR 1.01 (I recall 0.87 to 1.17) was cut off in the extract; unverified.
- Ferguson et al. 2009 (US replication of planned vs unplanned) was only found via secondary description; no numbers verified.
- The adjusted OR in Garnett 2020 was not retrieved.
- No RCT evidence found on the isolated effect of setting a quit date, or on pre-quit trigger-identification exercises.

## 7. Relapse and slips: timing, lapse-to-relapse, and relapse-prevention interventions

### Takeaway
Relapse is front-loaded (most unaided relapse within about the first 8 days) and a slip is a strong warning sign (older studies put lapse-to-relapse at roughly 85-95%), but classic behavioural relapse-prevention skills training has not been shown to work; only extended varenicline has moderate-certainty support.

### Cited Findings
- Among unaided quitters, most relapse occurs in the first 8 days; 3-5% remain abstinent at 6-12 months. — [Hughes 2004, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14678060/)
- "Approximately 85-95% of lapsers ultimately relapse", as stated by Kirchner, Shiffman & Wileyto 2012 citing Brandon, Tiffany & Baker 1986 and Kenford et al. 1994. Cigarette smokers in treatment studies from the 1980s-90s. — [Kirchner et al. 2012, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3296289)
- Kenford et al. 1994 (two nicotine-patch trials): smoking status in the first 2 weeks, especially week 2, robustly predicted status at end of treatment and at 6 months; any smoking in the first two weeks predicted short- and long-term failure. — described in [Kirchner et al. 2012, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3296289)
- In the same prospective study, self-blame, self-efficacy and guilt after the **first** lapse did not predict relapse; responses to the recurrent lapses that followed mattered more. — [Kirchner et al. 2012, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3296289)
- **Relapse-prevention interventions.** Livingstone-Banks et al. 2019, Cochrane CD003999.pub6 (28 October 2019; latest version found): 81 studies, 69,094 participants. Behavioural interventions that teach people to recognise high-risk situations and cope with them provided no worthwhile benefit in preventing relapse in assisted abstainers (moderate certainty). Extended varenicline helped prevent relapse (moderate certainty, unexplained heterogeneity). Extended NRT: low-certainty evidence of no benefit in assisted abstainers, some suggestion of benefit in unassisted abstainers. — [Cochrane CD003999.pub6 abstract PDF](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD003999.pub6/pdf/CDSR/CD003999/CD003999_abstract.pdf); [UCL Discovery record](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10086300)

### Inferences
- "85-95% of slips become relapse" is old, secondary-cited, and from treatment-seeking cigarette smokers. It is also demotivating if shown raw. Better use: internal product rationale for responding to a slip quickly, not an onboarding statistic.
- The Kirchner finding supports DayXero's slip handling in a specific way: the first slip is not decisive; what happens after it (further lapses) is. That is citable and consistent with a non-punitive slip flow.
- The app should not claim its trigger-identification or coping features "prevent relapse" on the basis of evidence; Cochrane found no detectable effect of that class of intervention in people who had already quit. The honest claim is about the first week being the highest-risk period (Hughes 2004), which justifies daily check-ins early on.

### Gaps
- Numerical RRs from CD003999.pub6 (behavioural, extended varenicline, extended NRT) were not retrievable; I recall behavioural RR about 0.98 and varenicline about 1.23, unverified.
- Could not access the primary Brandon 1986, Kenford 1994 or Garvey 1992 papers; lapse-to-relapse percentages are via a secondary citation only.
- No modern (post-2015) relapse-curve dataset retrieved, and nothing on relapse timing for vaping or pouch cessation.

## 8. Commitment devices: pledges, incentives, implementation intentions

### Takeaway
Financial incentives have high-certainty evidence (Cochrane 2025), and "if-then" implementation intentions have a small supportive meta-analysis (OR 1.70) with heterogeneous, low absolute quit rates. I found no reliable evidence that a daily pledge by itself increases quitting.

### Cited Findings
- **Incentives.** Notley et al. 2025, Cochrane CD004307.pub7 (13 January 2025): high-certainty evidence that incentives improve cessation at long-term follow-up (6+ months) in mixed-population studies (48 studies, 21,924 participants), with the benefit persisting after incentives stop. Sensitivity analysis excluding 7 studies that still offered incentives at long-term follow-up: RR 1.46 (95% CI 1.23 to 1.73; 32 studies; 15,082 participants). — [Cochrane CD004307.pub7](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD004307.pub7/information); [UEA repository record](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/98253)
- Same review: evidence for incentives in pregnant people who smoke upgraded from moderate to high certainty, at end of pregnancy and postpartum. — [UEA repository record](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/98253)
- Cochrane 2021 network meta-analysis: guaranteed financial incentives OR 1.46 (95% CrI 1.15 to 1.85), high certainty. — [drugsandalcohol.ie record of CD013229](https://www.drugsandalcohol.ie/33632/)
- **Implementation intentions.** McWilliams, Bellhouse, Yorke, Lloyd & Armitage 2019, Health Psychology, "Beyond 'planning': a meta-analysis of implementation intentions to support smoking cessation": 12 studies; OR 1.70 (95% CI 1.32 to 2.20) for quitting at follow-up; average quit rate 10.7% with implementation intentions vs 4.9% in controls. Stand-alone implementation-intention interventions OR 5.69 (95% CI 1.39 to 23.25), a very wide interval; within multicomponent interventions OR 1.67. Substantial heterogeneity. — [PubMed](https://www.pubmed.gov/31414843/); [accepted manuscript](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/154864/)

### Inferences
- If-then planning is the best-evidenced lightweight commitment technique for an app that already does trigger identification: "when [trigger], I will [response]". Quote it cautiously: 12 small, heterogeneous studies, varying follow-up, and not Cochrane-graded.
- The incentive evidence is about **money or vouchers paid for verified abstinence**. Streaks, badges and pledges are not incentives in this sense, so the RR must not be attached to gamification.
- A daily pledge is best described as a design choice loosely grounded in commitment/implementation-intention theory, not as an evidence-based component.

### Gaps
- The headline pooled RR for incentives in the 2025 Cochrane update (I recall about 1.52, CI roughly 1.33 to 1.74) and the pregnancy RR were not verifiable; only the sensitivity-analysis RR 1.46 is confirmed. A news summary gave about 13 vs 6 per 100 quitting in pregnant participants, but that is a secondary source.
- The CI printed for the multicomponent implementation-intention OR 1.67 in the indexed abstract (1.29 to 1.66) does not contain the point estimate, so it is probably a typo; check the paper before quoting.
- No trial or review found on abstinence pledges, daily pledges or deposit contracts specifically within apps. Reliable sources on pledges alone: none found.

## 9. Applicability to vapers and nicotine-pouch users

### Takeaway
Everything in sections 1-8 is cigarette-smoker evidence. For quitting vaping the evidence is new and low-certainty; for nicotine pouches I found no cessation evidence at all.

### Cited Findings
- Cochrane living review "Interventions for quitting vaping" (Butler, Hartmann-Boyce et al.; first published January 2025; 9 studies, just over 5,000 participants): text-message support may help more young people aged 13-24 stop vaping than no/minimal support, and varenicline may help adults stop vaping; both low certainty. The text-message trials used the same programme, so results may not transfer. The authors do not know whether other interventions help people stop vaping for 6 months or more, and want more data on harms including return to smoking. — [Cochrane CD016058](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD016058); [Oxford CEBM briefing, January 2025](https://www.cebm.ox.ac.uk/files/reports/iqv-briefing-hcp-and-policy-makers-jan-2025.pdf)
- A later update of the same living review (described by Oxford CEBM as the November 2025 version) included 15 studies. — [Oxford CEBM briefing](https://www.cebm.ox.ac.uk/files/resources/iqv-briefing-hcp-and-policy-makers-july-2026.pdf/view)
- The WHO 2024 guideline covers cigarettes, waterpipes, smokeless tobacco, cigars, roll-your-own and heated tobacco products, and includes a section on smokeless tobacco cessation. — [WHO 2024](https://www.who.int/news/item/02-07-2024-who-releases-first-ever-clinical-treatment-guideline-for-tobacco-cessation-in-adults)

### Inferences
- Onboarding statistics should be branched by product. Cigarette users can be shown the Cochrane NRT / behavioural figures. Vape and pouch users should see either a clearly labelled "evidence from people quitting cigarettes" caveat or no numeric claim.
- There is no evidence base for NRT taper schedules as a method to quit vaping or pouches; NRT labels are written for smoking cessation.

### Gaps
- Did not retrieve the effect sizes or the 2025/2026 updated conclusions of the vaping living review.
- No review or trial found on cessation of oral nicotine pouches. The Cochrane smokeless-tobacco cessation review (older, snus/chewing tobacco) was not retrieved, and the WHO guideline's specific smokeless-tobacco recommendations were not read.
- No data found on unaided quit rates, number of attempts, or relapse curves for vapers or pouch users.
